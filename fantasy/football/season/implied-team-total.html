The Season

Implied team total

One free, automatically-updating number that says how many points the betting market expects a specific team to score in a specific game — and the backbone of everything you do in-season.

Why this note is the backbone

Every other in-season note leans on this one. Start/sit decisions, kicker streaming, defence streaming, and reading which of a team's players will get the ball all resolve to the same number, and it takes about four seconds to look up.

That matters more for you than for a normal manager. You are not going to watch games. You are not going to form a view on whether an offensive line is holding up or a quarterback looks off. This one number is your substitute for the film study you are not going to do — and it is not a cheap substitute, because it is the aggregated opinion of everyone who is doing that work and betting money on the answer.

It is also honest about what it is. An implied team total is a market expectation, not a projection anyone at ESPN produced and not an observed result. It is the price, not the outcome.

The two numbers you start from

Sportsbooks publish two numbers per game, and both are free to read on any odds page without an account.

  • The total (also called the over/under): the combined points both teams are expected to score. A typical NFL game total sits in the mid-40s.
  • The spread: how many points one team is expected to win by. The favourite is the team expected to win; the underdog is the other one. A spread of 3 means the favourite is expected to win by three.

See Fantasy football glossary for both.

The arithmetic, in one sentence

Take half the total. Add half the spread for the favourite; subtract half the spread for the underdog.

That is the whole thing. Three worked examples:

Total 47, favourite by 3. Half the total is 23.5. Half the spread is 1.5. So the market expects the favourite to score about 25 and the underdog about 22. Add them back: 47. Subtract them: 3. Both check out.

Total 54, favourite by 7. Half the total is 27. Half the spread is 3.5. The favourite is at 30.5, the underdog at 23.5. That favourite is one of the best offensive environments on the board that week — and note that the underdog here, at 23.5, is also above average. A high total lifts both teams.

Total 38, favourite by 6. Half the total is 19. Half the spread is 3. The favourite is at 22, the underdog at 16. That 16 is a team the market expects to do almost nothing.

And a pick'em, where neither team is favoured: total 44, spread zero, both teams at 22. Half the total, no adjustment.

(These four are invented illustrations. As of 2026-07-25 no 2026 weekly game lines are posted; books publish them roughly a week out.)

Rough calibration, from where NFL totals have sat in recent seasons: an average team's implied total is a little over 22. Call 27 or more a strong environment, 20 or less a bad one, and everything between them noise.

Use one: the FLEX

Your FLEX is the one lineup slot that takes a running back, receiver or tight end interchangeably. It is one of only three live weekly decisions you have — see Start-sit — a standing rule, not a research project.

When two FLEX candidates are close on ability, start the one whose team has the higher implied total. More expected points for his offence means more expected scoring plays for him to be part of. This is a tiebreaker, not an override: it does not tell you to bench a genuinely better player.

Use two: streaming the kicker

Streaming means rotating a roster spot weekly by matchup instead of holding one player — a concept you already own from basketball, with one difference: every team plays exactly once a week here, so streaming buys matchup quality only, never extra games.

Kickers are the purest streaming target in the sport, because a kicker's fantasy scoring is a property of the offence he plays for, not of him. He cannot attempt a field goal his team does not drive him into range for. So you do not stream by kicker name. You stream by implied team total: take whichever available kicker's offence is expected to score most.

One honest wrinkle: an extremely high implied total partly means touchdowns, and a touchdown ends a drive with no field goal attempt. The sweet spot is a good offence that stalls, which you cannot see in advance. The total is still the best single filter available — just do not expect it to be tight. The last two rounds — kicker and defence covers the rest, including this league's minus one point for any missed kick.

Use three: streaming the defence — and be more aggressive than the advice you will read

For your D/ST (team defence and special teams, one roster slot), the number you care about is the opponent's implied total. Low opponent total, good week.

In this league, be far more aggressive about this than any published ranking suggests. Our scoring runs active yards-allowed brackets — a defence loses points for surrendering yardage, all the way down to minus seven for 550 or more. Virtually every ranking you can find online prices points allowed only. The downside of a genuinely bad matchup here is roughly double what those rankings assume: forty-six-plus points allowed is minus five, and if that came with 550 yards it is minus twelve before a single sack is counted.

The practical rule: never start a defence against a good offence, whatever the ranking says, and be happy starting an unremarkable defence against a team implied for under 20.

Game script: not just how many points, but who gets them

Game script is how a game is expected to unfold on the scoreboard. It matters because coaches respond to it in a predictable way: a team that is winning runs the ball to burn clock; a team that is losing throws, because throwing is the only way to score quickly.

So the spread tells you two things at once. The implied total tells you how big the pie is; the spread tells you how it gets sliced. A heavy favourite's running back gets a fatter share of carries, especially late. A heavy underdog's receivers get a fatter share of throws. See Opportunity share.

A basketball false friend, and it is a sharp one. In basketball, a blowout is a dead zone — your player gets pulled, the bench mob plays, and the minutes you were counting on evaporate. Football inverts this. Nobody rests when they are losing. The trailing team's starters stay on the field and throw, against a defence that has stopped caring about yards. That is garbage time, and it is fully counted in fantasy. Being blown out produces real fantasy points for the losing team's pass-catchers. Do not bench a receiver because his team is expected to lose badly. Bench him because his team is expected to score nothing at all — those are different games.

Open questions

  • Implied totals are derived from prices that move all week on injury news and money. A Wednesday reading and a Sunday-morning reading can differ meaningfully; the Sunday one is the one that counts.
  • The share of a team's total that reaches any individual player is not in this number at all. It tells you the size of the pie and nothing about the slice — that is what Opportunity share is for.
  • The calibration thresholds here (27 high, 20 low) are rules of thumb from recent seasons' totals, not measured against 2026 lines, which do not exist yet as of 2026-07-25.
