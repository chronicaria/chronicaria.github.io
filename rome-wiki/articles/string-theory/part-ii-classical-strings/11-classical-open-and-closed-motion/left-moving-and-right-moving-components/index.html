    Left-moving and right-moving components

    Every motion of a classical string is two waveforms sliding past each other — one to the left, one to the right, each frozen in shape and moving at speed c : \vec X(t,\sigma)=\tfrac12\big(\vec F(ct+\sigma)+\vec G(ct-\sigma)\big) .

    Pluck a guitar string and the bump you made does not sit still — it splits into two half-height bumps that travel in opposite directions. The string's shape at any instant is the sum of a left-traveller and a right-traveller, each rigid, each gliding at the wave speed ccc. Nothing about the shapes themselves is fixed yet; the boundary conditions (open vs closed) come later and decide how the two travellers are related. This note establishes only that the split exists and what the parameterization constraints demand of each half. The one non-obvious ingredient is that the wave operator factorizes into a left-derivative times a right-derivative; once it does, the general solution is immediate.

    Throughout, X⃗˙=∂tX⃗\dot{\vec X}=\partial_t\vec XX˙=∂t​X and X⃗′=∂σX⃗\vec X'=\partial_\sigma\vec XX′=∂σ​X, while a prime on F⃗\vec FF or G⃗\vec GG is the derivative in its single argument.

    Which is left- and which is right-moving

    A "waveform moving at speed ccc" means the same value of F⃗\vec FF reappears wherever its argument is held constant. Follow a point of constant argument ct+σ=constct+\sigma=\text{const}ct+σ=const: dσdt=−c,\frac{d\sigma}{dt}=-c,dtdσ​=−c, so the whole pattern F⃗(ct+σ)\vec F(ct+\sigma)F(ct+σ) slides toward smaller σ\sigmaσ — call that "left." Likewise G⃗(ct−σ)\vec G(ct-\sigma)G(ct−σ) has constant argument along dσ/dt=+cd\sigma/dt=+cdσ/dt=+c, sliding toward larger σ\sigmaσ — "right." Hence:

    • F⃗(ct+σ)\vec F(ct+\sigma)F(ct+σ) — left-mover.
    • G⃗(ct−σ)\vec G(ct-\sigma)G(ct−σ) — right-mover.

    Both travel at speed ccc in the σ\sigmaσ coordinate, exactly as a relativistic string demands.

    What the constraints say about each mover

    The wave equation alone knows nothing of the energy parameterization; that information lives in the two constraints, not yet imposed. Differentiate X⃗=12(F⃗+G⃗)\vec X=\tfrac12(\vec F+\vec G)X=21​(F+G): X⃗′=12(F⃗′(u+)−G⃗′(u−)),1cX⃗˙=12(F⃗′(u+)+G⃗′(u−)).\vec X'=\tfrac12\big(\vec F'(u_+)-\vec G'(u_-)\big),\qquad \frac1c\dot{\vec X}=\tfrac12\big(\vec F'(u_+)+\vec G'(u_-)\big).X′=21​(F′(u+​)−G′(u−​)),c1​X˙=21​(F′(u+​)+G′(u−​)). Adding and subtracting isolates each mover cleanly: \vec X'+\frac1c\dot{\vec X}=\vec F'(u_+),\qquad \vec X'-\frac1c\dot{\vec X}=-\vec G'(u_-). \tag{2} The two parameterization constraints, added and subtracted, package into the single statement (derived in Wave equation and constraints) (X⃗′±1cX⃗˙)2=1.\left(\vec X'\pm\frac1c\dot{\vec X}\right)^2 = 1.(X′±c1​X˙)2=1.     Be careful about what is and is not fixed here. It is the derivative F⃗′\vec F'F′ that is a unit vector, never F⃗\vec FF itself. The curve F⃗(u)\vec F(u)F(u) may wander anywhere in space, loop, or double back; only the speed at which it is traced is pinned to 111. All the physics of a specific string lives in the shapes of these two unit-speed curves.

    Where this goes next

    The boundary conditions now break the symmetry between the two movers:

    • Open string — the free (Neumann) ends act as mirrors, reflecting the right-mover into the left-mover, so G⃗\vec GG is tied to F⃗\vec FF and there is really one independent waveform (Open-string reflection at endpoints).
    • Closed string — no ends, no mirrors; F⃗\vec FF and G⃗\vec GG stay independent, the classical seed of the closed string's two separate towers of oscillators (Closed-string periodicity and rotating strings).

    That split — one waveform versus two — is the entire difference between open and closed classical motion.

    Exercise

    A pure left-mover X⃗(t,σ)=12F⃗(ct+σ)\vec X(t,\sigma)=\tfrac12\vec F(ct+\sigma)X(t,σ)=21​F(ct+σ) — that is, G⃗≡0\vec G\equiv 0G≡0 in equation (1) — solves the wave equation for any F⃗\vec FF.     Solution

    The lesson: the constraints are not optional dressing on the wave equation — they require both movers to be present. In general X⃗˙⋅X⃗′=c4(∣F⃗′∣2−∣G⃗′∣2)\dot{\vec X}\cdot\vec X'=\tfrac{c}{4}\big(|\vec F'|^2-|\vec G'|^2\big)X˙⋅X′=4c​(∣F′∣2−∣G′∣2), so the string's velocity is orthogonal to its tangent exactly when the two movers are traced at equal speeds — which the unit-speed condition (3) enforces automatically. With a single mover, velocity (∝F⃗′+G⃗′\propto\vec F'+\vec G'∝F′+G′) and tangent (∝F⃗′−G⃗′\propto\vec F'-\vec G'∝F′−G′) become parallel, and their dot product c4∣F⃗′∣2\tfrac{c}{4}|\vec F'|^24c​∣F′∣2 cannot vanish for a nontrivial string. This is why the boundary conditions never delete a mover outright: open strings reflect G⃗\vec GG into F⃗\vec FF, and closed strings keep both — neither ever sets one to zero.
