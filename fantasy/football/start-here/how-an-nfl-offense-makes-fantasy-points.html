How an NFL offense makes fantasy points

An NFL team runs a fixed number of plays each week and hands them to six or seven people, so fantasy football is a fight over the division of a pie, not over talent.

Start with the zero-sum fact

An NFL team throws the ball about 33 times a game and runs it about 26 times. That is roughly 59 scoring opportunities, and they get divided among one quarterback, one or two running backs, two or three receivers, and one tight end. Six or seven people. Nobody else on the field is eligible for meaningful volume.

Everything follows from that. A target — a pass thrown in a player's direction, counted whether or not he catches it — that goes to the number-two receiver is a target the number-one receiver did not get. A carry handed to the backup is a carry the starter did not get. There is no version of the week where both of them get it.

This is why you will see this build talk constantly about share and almost never about how good someone is. Talent decides who gets the touches in August. After that the touches are the thing, and they are conserved. See The conserved team touch budget and Opportunity share.

What a game actually consists of

The team with the ball gets four attempts — four downs — to advance ten yards. Make the ten and you get a fresh set of four. Fail and you hand the ball over, usually by punting on the fourth attempt — kicking the ball far downfield so the other team at least starts a long way back — rather than risking a turnover, which is losing the ball to the defence where you stand.

A possession from getting the ball to giving it up is a drive. Each team gets about eleven drives a game, and runs about 60 to 65 plays across them. Some of those plays are sacks — the quarterback tackled behind the line before he can throw — and penalties, which score nothing for anyone, which is why 60–65 plays produces only ~59 useful ones.

Each play begins with a snap — the ball put into play — and resolves one of two ways. A run: the quarterback hands off, and the carry belongs to whoever takes it. A pass: the quarterback throws, and the target belongs to whoever it was thrown at. That is the whole machine. Fantasy points are just an accounting layer on top of it.

How plays turn into points

Under this league's full PPR rules — PPR means every reception is worth a full point on its own, before a single yard:

  • Rushing and receiving yards pay 0.1 per yard, so ten yards is one point.
  • Passing yards pay 0.04 per yard, so twenty-five yards is one point. A quarterback's yards are worth less than a fifth of a receiver's, per yard.
  • Every catch pays 1.0, whatever the yardage.
  • A rushing or receiving touchdown pays 6. A touchdown pass pays the quarterback 4.

Round numbers, as an illustration and not a projection: a quarterback throwing 33 times for 230 yards with two touchdown passes scores about 9 points of yardage plus 8 of touchdowns — around 17. A running back with 15 carries for 65 yards and 3 catches for 25 scores 6.5 plus 3 catch points plus 2.5 — about 12. The receiver who catches 6 of his 9 targets for 70 yards scores 6 for the catches plus 7 for the yards — about 13, and the three incompletions paid nothing but still consumed three of the team's 33 throws.

Note what that last line does. Volume is the input; efficiency is the multiplier. A player who is targeted ten times and is mediocre outscores a player who is targeted four times and is excellent, almost every week.

The red zone is where it stops being linear

The red zone is the last twenty yards before the end zone. It matters out of all proportion to its size, because of one piece of arithmetic:

A touchdown is 6 points. Yards are 1 point per 10 yards. So a touchdown is worth 60 yards.

A back who takes the ball in from the two-yard line scores 6.2 points on that play. To earn the same from ordinary work he would need 62 rushing yards — most of a good day. This is why a player with a modest yardage total and the short-yardage job can outscore a far better player without it, and it is why "who gets the ball inside the five" is one of the few pieces of coaching detail worth knowing.

It is also the main source of week-to-week noise. Touchdowns are rare events landing on a small number of touches, so they swing scores violently and do not repeat reliably. See Touchdown variance before you draft anyone whose case is mostly touchdowns.

Game script, in one line

Game script is the shape a game takes once one team is ahead. A leading team runs the ball to burn clock; a trailing team throws to catch up. So a blowout inflates the losing team's receivers and the winning team's running back, and deflates the reverse. Late points scored in a decided game are garbage time — they count fully in fantasy, which is a real and slightly absurd source of value.

The false friend from basketball

In basketball, a hot player takes more shots. The game reallocates in real time toward whoever is producing, and usage is partly self-earned.

Football does not do this. The distribution of touches is decided by a coach before the play, not by the player during it. A receiver can be open all afternoon and finish with two targets. A back can average six yards a carry and get eight carries. Usage is an assignment, and it is far stickier and far less merit-responsive than a basketball usage rate. Buy the assignment. See What basketball teaches you wrong about football.

Open questions

  • The 33 passes / 26 carries split is a league-wide average shape, not a measured 2026 figure; individual offenses vary a lot, and pace and pass-lean per team live in Teams/The 32 offenses rather than here.
  • Play counts drift year to year with rule and pace changes. The 60–65 range is a durable order of magnitude, not a number to compute with.
  • How much red-zone role is actually forecastable before Week 1 is genuinely unclear — the touchdown share is the least stable input in the whole model.
