Who to take at 1.01

Five players are close enough at the top of the board that the first pick is a tiebreaker exercise, not a discovery.

This is the single most perishable note in the build

Written from **2026-07-25 consensus**. It **expires 2026-08-15** and **must be rewritten in the 72 hours before the draft before it is used.** Nothing else here decays this fast. A note about replacement level is still true in November; this one is a snapshot of seven players' projected stat lines in one week of July. Camp opens, depth charts resolve at the August 30 cutdown, someone gets hurt in preseason — and this is not slightly wrong, it is pointing at the wrong player. If it is draft night and this has not been rewritten since 2026-07-25, **do not use it.** Re-run `Data/build_board.py`, re-read the top rows, redo the tiebreakers on fresh numbers.

VOR — value over replacement — is projected points above the last startable player at the same position; see Value over replacement in fantasy football and Fantasy football glossary.

The arithmetic is the whole argument. Rank 1 to rank 3 is 21 points across a 17-game season, about 1.2 a week; rank 1 down to rank 7 is 59 points, about 3.5 a week. One touchdown is 6 points — so first to seventh is roughly ten touchdowns spread over seventeen weeks, and first to third about three and a half.

That is smaller than the error in any preseason projection. These are a model's central guess at a stat line six weeks before kickoff, and no system is accurate to within three touchdowns a year on one player. The differences are real in the spreadsheet and meaningless on the field. Treat the top five as interchangeable on projected points and decide on the tiebreakers — one of which, availability, now separates them properly. Ranks 1 and 2 are literally tied — 367.4 and 366.9. Do not let the ordering pretend that is a decision.

The candidates

Three sentences each. Wanting more confidence than that in July means manufacturing it. Archetypes are from the closed set in Player archetypes.

Jahmyr Gibbs (DET). A three-down bell cow — on the field every down, collecting both carries and catches, which stacks two scoring streams on one roster spot. The case: 279 carries plus 72 catches projected, and in full PPR — every reception worth one point, see Fantasy football glossary — those catches are 72 points before a yard is gained. The problem: he is the only one in this group flagged med risk rather than low — though the games-played record does not back that label up, and criterion 4 below shows why.

Bijan Robinson (ATL). Also a three-down bell cow: 280 carries plus 84 catches, the highest receiving volume of any back on the board. The case: catches accrue every week while touchdowns arrive in lumps, so those 84 are the most stable points at the position (see Touchdown variance). The problem: his bye is week 11, the worst week of the 2026 calendar with six teams off.

Christian McCaffrey (SF). A third three-down bell cow: 279 carries plus 82 catches, 346 points, 21 behind the top two. The case: nearly identical receiving usage to Robinson, and a week 8 bye, clear of the week-11 crunch. The problem: the projection docks him 21 points without showing why, and the two things that could explain it are both now on the record — he turns 30.3 on opening day, three years older than anyone else here, and 2024 is the only near-lost season in the group. Criterion 4 has the numbers.

Puka Nacua (LAR). An alpha X — his team's clear first option, lined up wide, targeted regardless of matchup. The case: 116 catches for 1,561 yards projected, the most receiving yards on the board, and target volume is the most stable input in fantasy football. The problem: 28 points below the top, and also a week 11 bye.

Ja'Marr Chase (CIN). Another alpha X: 116 catches for 1,474 yards, 330 points, same low risk flag. The problem: 37 points back, and a week 6 bye — one of five weeks tied at four teams off, so a nuisance rather than a crunch.

Amon-Ra St. Brown (DET) and Jaxon Smith-Njigba (SEA) sit just behind at 318 and 308 points, both alpha X; St. Brown has the most projected catches on the board at 119, Smith-Njigba the higher yardage at 1,521. Neither is a mistake, but the cost against the top of the board is real and the two are not the same: St. Brown is 49 points back, Smith-Njigba 59, for no tiebreaker gain — they answer "who is left at 1.24?", not this question.

The tiebreakers, in this order

Run them in order and stop when one resolves.

1. Full-PPR receiving volume. Every reception is a full point before yards, so projected catches are the most decision-relevant number here — and the most stable, since a target share is a coaching decision that repeats weekly. Among the backs: Robinson 84, McCaffrey 82, Gibbs 72. Leans Robinson, narrowly over McCaffrey; Gibbs trails by a dozen points on the most reliable input.

2. The two-stage payout — high mean, not swing. Weeks 1–14 you must finish top 6 of 12 just to survive; only then does the convex prize structure reward variance. Rounds 1–4 protect the mean, round 7 onward buys upside — see How the league is won. Gibbs is the group's only med risk, but that is a one-word label with no history attached to it. Argued against Gibbs in the first draft of this note. Criterion 4 now overrules it — of the three backs, his record is second only to Robinson's.

3. Bye week, against the roster you will own. A bye is a week a player scores zero because his team is off. Your picks are 1, then 24 and 25 — a 22-pick blind window, so the roster is unknown here, but the calendar is not: week 11 has six teams off, and weeks 6, 7, 8, 10 and 13 are tied at four each. Robinson, Nacua and Smith-Njigba are week 11 — the only genuine crunch in the group. Gibbs, Chase and St. Brown are week 6 and McCaffrey week 8, which are the same four-team nuisance as each other. Argues against the three week-11 players rather than for any one name — and one week in fourteen is a smaller effect than criterion 1. Do not let it override.

4. Availability — now measured, and it decides this. This used to be the criterion that abstained. It is now on file. Four things fall out of that table.

Robinson has played every game of his career — 51 of 51 — and has never once appeared on the official weekly injury report in three seasons, not even as Questionable. Nobody else in the group is clean on both counts. That turns criterion 1's narrow lean into an actual answer.

The med flag on Gibbs is not about games missed. He has played 49 of 51, missed one week listed Out, and the only body part on his three-year report is a hamstring, twice. Whatever the label encodes, the record does not show a player who breaks down. Criterion 2's case against him dissolves.

McCaffrey is the real risk, and it is the one the board's plain VOR hides. Fourteen of 51 games missed, thirteen of them in 2024 when he played four of seventeen, and he is 30.3 on opening day against 24.6 for Robinson. Read the report line carefully, though: only two of those fourteen games came in weeks he was listed Out. The file counts the other twelve as absences with no injury-report designation — and that column is a residual, games missed minus weeks listed Out, so treat it as "not on the weekly report", not as a diagnosis.

Being listed is not the same as missing. St. Brown was Questionable or Doubtful for seven weeks and missed one game in three years. Nacua was Questionable or Doubtful for seven weeks and missed seven games. The designation tells you a body part is being managed; only the games column tells you whether it cost you a start. See Reading the NFL injury report.

Resolves toward Robinson, and moves McCaffrey out of the conversation.

Where the numbers change the ranking

The 2026 draft board carries an Adj VOR column — value over replacement after each projection is scaled by that player's availability relative to his position's mean. It is a model, and it is explained in Value over replacement in fantasy football. Ordered by it, the top seven of the board is the same seven players in a different order:

Ordering The top seven, in order
By VOR Gibbs · Robinson · McCaffrey · Nacua · Chase · St. Brown · Smith-Njigba
By Adj VOR Robinson · Gibbs · Chase · St. Brown · Smith-Njigba · Nacua · McCaffrey

Robinson passes Gibbs. McCaffrey falls from third to seventh — from a top-three pick to behind all four receivers — and Nacua slides from fourth to sixth. The rest of the top of the board holds. That is a model reordering a model, not a fact about September; but it is the tiebreaker this note previously had to leave blank, and it points the same way criteria 1 and 2 already did.

Where that lands, 2026-07-25: criteria 1 and 4 both point at Bijan Robinson; criterion 2 has stopped arguing against Gibbs, since the label it rested on has no record behind it; criterion 3 leans mildly against Robinson on the week-11 bye and toward Christian McCaffrey — and criterion 4 answers that too, because the bye costs one week and McCaffrey has missed fourteen. Robinson, and no longer narrowly. The gap in projected points is still about a touchdown a month, so if on draft night you prefer Gibbs for a reason July cannot see, take him and do not agonise. Taking McCaffrey now needs a better reason than it did a week ago.

Why the backs sit above the receivers at all

Partly because of the method, and you should know how much.

The board ranks by VOR: projected points minus what the last startable player at that position gives you. This league starts roughly 29 running backs a week against 31 receivers, and the drop from the best back to the 29th is steeper than the equivalent drop at receiver, so backs collect more VOR from the same raw points. Computed from this CSV on 2026-07-25, the per-week edge of the best player at each position over replacement — the last player you could still start, which is the 29th back and the 31st receiver but only the 12th tight end and 12th quarterback — is RB 10.7 · WR 8.5 · TE 5.7 · QB 4.4. Measured against the 12th-best at the position instead, the back and receiver figures are 6.3 and 5.9: the running back edge shrinks but does not vanish. The scarcity is measured, not asserted — see Tiers by position.

The caveat is smaller than this note first claimed. Plain VOR still compares totals as if everyone plays all 17 games — every row above is projected for 17 — so it rewards backs for being scarce without charging anyone for missing time. But the second half of the old caveat, that running back is the most injury-prone position, is not what the data says. Across 2023–2025, among the players actually on this board, backs lost 27% of their seasons to four or more missed games, against 32% for tight ends, 29% for quarterbacks and 24% for receivers, and their rate of near-lost seasons — eight games or fewer — is 6%, the joint lowest with tight end and well under the quarterbacks' 16%. On none of the four measures in Availability and injury risk is running back the worst position; the one that is consistently worst is quarterback. Observed games played, from Data/player_context.csv, retrieved 2026-07-25; full tables in that note.

Two honest limits. The pool is defined by 2026 projection, so it is survivor-selected: a back whose career was ended by injury is not on the board to be counted. And what separates backs from receivers is marginal rather than categorical: receivers hold a slightly higher mean availability, 0.861 against 0.857, while backs are the position least likely to lose at least one game in a season, 63% against the receivers' 67%. So "the board says RB" is closer to "RB is correct" than it was, and the gap criterion 4 could not close is now closed by measurement. McCaffrey is a fact about McCaffrey, not about running backs.

Open questions

  • Availability now decides this, but it is measured backwards. Three seasons is a thin sample for a rate, and nothing in Avail knows about a hamstring pulled in August camp — which is the reason this note still has to be rewritten in the 72 hours before the draft.
  • The 21-point gap between the top two and McCaffrey is still asserted by the projection with no explanation attached. Age 30.3 and a four-game 2024 are the obvious candidates, but I cannot confirm the projection is pricing either of them rather than something else.
  • ADP here is Fantasy Football Calculator PPR 12-team, a labelled proxy for ESPN's own, which could not be extracted programmatically. If your league drafts closer to ESPN's board than to the mock market, the "who is left at 1.24" reasoning shifts.
