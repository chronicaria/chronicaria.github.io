<p>Classically this set is called the <strong>primitive equations</strong>, which assume hydrostatic balance in the vertical. That label is now slightly dated: the leading operational cores (ECMWF IFS, ICON, GFS-FV3, Met Office UM) are <strong>non-hydrostatic</strong>, so they go beyond the classical primitive set. The continuous equations are discretized onto a grid (or spectral basis) and marched forward in timesteps.</p>
<h2 id="where-the-initial-state-comes-from-data-assimilation">Where the initial state comes from: data assimilation</h2>
<p>A forecast is only as good as its starting point. <strong>Data assimilation</strong> blends a short model forecast (the "background") with fresh observations to produce the best estimate of the current atmosphere. ECMWF uses <strong>4D-Var</strong>: it receives ~800 million observations/day and assimilates ~60 million quality-controlled ones, the vast majority from <strong>satellites</strong> (~90 instruments). This is the step whose residual error seeds all later <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/weather/atmospheric-predictability-limit/" class="wiki-link">chaotic error growth</a>.</p>

<p>ECMWF's IFS also drives the <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/weather/ensemble-forecasting/" class="wiki-link">ensemble (ENS)</a>, now at the same ~9 km resolution. Machine-learning emulators (GraphCast, Pangu-Weather, ECMWF's AIFS) now match or beat these physics models on some <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/weather/forecast-skill/" class="wiki-link">skill scores</a> at a fraction of the compute — an active shift as of 2026.</p>
