17:T1d7a,<h2 id="chaos-sensitive-dependence-on-initial-conditions">Chaos: sensitive dependence on initial conditions</h2>
<p>Edward <strong>Lorenz (1963)</strong>, studying a toy convection model, found <em>deterministic nonperiodic flow</em>: trajectories that never repeat and diverge exponentially from nearly identical starts. Tiny errors in the initial state amplify without bound. He dramatized it in a 1972 talk — <em>"Does the Flap of a Butterfly's Wings in Brazil Set Off a Tornado in Texas?"</em> — giving us the <strong>butterfly effect</strong>.</p>
<p>The deeper result is <strong>Lorenz (1969)</strong>: a flow with <em>many scales of motion</em> is only finitely predictable. Two states differing initially by a small observational error evolve into states <strong>as different as two randomly chosen states within a finite time</strong> — and, crucially, <em>shrinking the initial error does not push that horizon out proportionally</em>. This is why you can't just measure harder to forecast a month out.</p>

<h2 id="the-number-2-weeks">The number: ~2 weeks</h2>
<p><strong>Zhang et al. (2019)</strong> put a value on it. Their finding, near-verbatim: a skillful lead time for midlatitude instantaneous weather is <em>currently ~10 days</em> (the <strong>practical</strong> limit); reducing initial-condition uncertainty by an order of magnitude, with a near-perfect model, extends deterministic forecasts by <em>at most ~5 more days</em>. So the <strong>intrinsic</strong> limit is roughly <strong>two weeks</strong>, and current systems sit ~4–5 days short of it.</p>
<p>Beyond that ceiling, a deterministic daily forecast is no better than climatology — which is exactly where <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/weather/temperature-forecast-confidence-intervals/" class="wiki-link">Temperature forecast confidence intervals</a> widen to the climatological spread, and why longer-range prediction goes <strong>probabilistic</strong> (<a href="/rome-wiki/articles/weather/ensemble-forecasting/" class="wiki-link">Ensemble forecasting</a>) and sub-seasonal.</p>
