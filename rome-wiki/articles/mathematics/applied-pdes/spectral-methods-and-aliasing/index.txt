<p>The Nyquist endpoint needs a convention. <p>Viscosity may mask this at adequate resolution, but it does not logically remove the error. <h2 id="exact-and-approximate-remedies">Exact and approximate remedies</h2>
<h3 id="modal-truncation-and-sharp-filtering">Modal truncation and sharp filtering</h3>

style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">&#x3C;</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.7667em;vertical-align:-0.0833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">N</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span><span class="mbin">−</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" Endpoint details depend on parity and FFT convention.</p>
<p>The <strong>2/3 rule</strong> is exact for the retained quadratic convolution under these assumptions, but it gives up about one third of the one-dimensional bandwidth—and a much larger fraction of tensor-product modes in several dimensions. Sharp cutoffs can also produce ringing in physical space. Smooth exponential or raised-cosine filters avoid a hard spectral edge and often improve robustness, but they are intentionally dissipative and generally do not eliminate every alias exactly. Their order, cutoff, and application frequency become model parameters that require convergence tests.</p>
<h3 id="the-32-padding-rule">The 3/2 padding rule</h3>
<p>Padding preserves the original retained bandwidth. Suppose the desired field uses <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>N</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">N</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">N</span></span></span></span> Fourier coefficients. To form a quadratic product:</p>
<ol>
<li>embed both spectra in a zero-padded array of about <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>M</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>3</mn><mi>N</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">/</mi><mn>2</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">M=3N/2</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">M</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">=</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord">3</span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">N</span><span class="mord">/2</span></span></span></span> modes;</li>
<li>inverse-transform on the <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>M</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">M</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">M</span></span></span></span>-point grid;</li>
<li>multiply pointwise there;</li>
<li>transform back at size <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>M</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">M</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">M</span></span></span></span>;</li>
<li>truncate to the original <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>N</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">N</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">N</span></span></span></span> modes.</li>
</ol>
<p>Why <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mn>3</mn><mi mathvariant="normal">/</mi><mn>2</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">3/2</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord">3/2</span></span></span></span>? If the original cutoff is approximately <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>K</mi><mo>=</mo><mi>N</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">/</mi><mn>2</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">K=N/2</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0715em;">K</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">=</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">N</span><span class="mord">/2</span></span></span></span>, quadratic output reaches <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mn>2</mn><mi>K</mi><mo>≈</mo><mi>N</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">2K\approx N</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord">2</span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0715em;">K</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">≈</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">N</span></span></span></span>. On the padded grid, copies are separated by <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>M</mi><mo>≈</mo><mn>3</mn><mi>N</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">/</mi><mn>2</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">M\approx3N/2</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">M</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">≈</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord">3</span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">N</span><span class="mord">/2</span></span></span></span>. Any folded component near <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>N</mi><mo>−</mo><mi>M</mi><mo>≈</mo><mo>−</mo><mi>N</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">/</mi><mn>2</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">N-M\approx-N/2</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.7667em;vertical-align:-0.0833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">N</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span><span class="mbin">−</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">M</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">≈</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord">−</span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">N</span><span class="mord">/2</span></span></span></span> lands at or beyond the boundary of the original retained band, so the interior convolution is recovered without contamination. Precise array lengths and endpoint zeroing again depend on odd/even conventions.</p>
<p>Padding computes the same retained coefficients as an exactly projected quadratic Galerkin convolution, up to floating-point error. It costs larger FFTs and work arrays. A code may pad only periodic directions and use overintegration in polynomial directions.</p>
<p>For a degree-<span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>r</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">r</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.4306em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0278em;">r</span></span></span></span> polynomial nonlinearity, the required oversampling grows. A useful Fourier counting rule is <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>M</mi><mo>≳</mo><mo xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>N</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">N</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">N</span></span></span></span> retained coefficients: <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mn>3</mn><mi>N</mi><mi <h3 id="overintegration-and-polynomial-spectral-elements">Overintegration and polynomial spectral elements</h3>
<p>Legendre or Chebyshev collocation has no simple modulo-<span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>N</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">N</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.109em;">N</span></span></span></span> Fourier wrap, but the same mechanism appears as inexact quadrature. If the trial solution has polynomial degree <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.1389em;">P</span></span></span></span>, a quadratic flux has degree up to <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mn>2</mn><mi>P</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">2P</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord">2</span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.1389em;">P</span></span></span></span>; testing it against a degree-<span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.1389em;">P</span></span></span></span> basis can produce an integrand of degree up to <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mn>3</mn><mi>P</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">3P</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord">3</span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.1389em;">P</span></span></span></span> before derivatives alter the count. A quadrature selected merely to integrate linear mass terms exactly need not integrate this nonlinear residual exactly.</p>
<p><strong>Overintegration</strong> evaluates the nonlinear term at more quadrature or “super-collocation” points and projects it back. Kirby and Karniadakis derive such rules from the exactness degree of the quadrature rather than copying the Fourier <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mn>3</mn><mi mathvariant="normal">/</mi><mn>2</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">3/2</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord">3/2</span></span></span></span> slogan blindly. Geometry, variable coefficients, rational fluxes, and equation-of-state evaluations can make the integrand nonpolynomial, so no finite quadrature order is universally exact. Then increasing quadrature is an error-control strategy, verified empirically, rather than a proof of zero aliasing.</p>

<h2 id="limitations-and-place-in-the-map">Limitations and place in the map</h2>
<p>Spectral dealiasing assumes enough regularity for a global high-order representation to be useful. After shock formation, a formally dealiased Fourier method still exhibits Gibbs oscillations and generally needs shock capturing, spectral viscosity, filtering, or a different discretization tied to <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/mathematics/applied-pdes/weak-solutions-and-why-shocks-require-them/" class="wiki-link">Weak solutions and why shocks require them</a> and <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/mathematics/applied-pdes/conservation-laws-and-entropy-conditions/" class="wiki-link">Conservation laws and entropy conditions</a>. Dealiasing prevents false wraparound; it does not supply the entropy selection mechanism.</p>
<p>Complex domains, discontinuous material coefficients, nonperiodic boundaries, and adaptive resolution favor spectral elements, <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/mathematics/applied-pdes/discontinuous-galerkin-methods/" class="wiki-link">Discontinuous Galerkin methods</a>, or other local schemes, where quadrature aliasing, geometric aliasing, and interface stability replace the simple Fourier story. On mapped meshes, inaccurate metric terms can violate free-stream preservation even when the volume nonlinearity is overintegrated. In turbulence, removing aliasing also does not prove that the smallest physical scales are resolved: a clean high-wavenumber spectrum can still be a numerically stabilized large-eddy calculation rather than direct numerical simulation.</p>
<p>The central lesson is narrower and stronger: nonlinear evaluation defines part of the numerical method. “Fourier differentiation plus an FFT product” is incomplete until the projection, sampling grid, alias treatment, invariant balance, and verification contract are specified.</p>
