The Draft

Drafting from the 1.01

Your slot is locked at pick 1, which buys you the single best player in the draft and then costs you twenty-two picks of silence — so the whole plan is a first pick that does not depend on the room, and a pair at 24 and 25 that is designed as one move.

The shape of the slot

A snake draft reverses each round, so the first pick of round one is the last pick of round two. Your sixteen picks are 1 · 24 · 25 · 48 · 49 · 72 · 73 · 96 · 97 · 120 · 121 · 144 · 145 · 168 · 169 · 192 — one pick at the very top, a gap of twenty-two, and then every remaining turn is two picks back to back.

Nobody else in this league has that as strongly. Slot 12 also picks back to back, but waits eleven picks for his first turn and never gets the best player on the board. You get both.

A bye is the one week each NFL team does not play, and a player on bye scores zero — see Bye-week planning.

A double-pick is not two picks. It is one decision with two outputs, which lets you do three things a single pick cannot:

  • Pair by position. Solve a starting requirement in one turn instead of gambling that a second receiver survives twelve more picks.
  • Pair safe with swing. Take the low-risk player first and the high-variance one second, so the turn has a floor and a ceiling.
  • Pair by bye week. Two starters who sit out the same week is a wound you can decline to inflict, at the moment you decide.

The twenty-two-pick blind window

After pick 1 you do not touch the draft again until pick 24. Twenty-two players come off the board and you react to none of them.

One clean consequence for pick 1: take the player whose value depends least on what happens next. Not the player who is best if the room behaves, and not a bargain relative to the market — at pick 1 there is no market to be a bargain against, because nobody has picked yet. Take the highest-floor version of the highest-value archetype and stop thinking.

A second, less obvious consequence for picks 24 and 25: you cannot plan them against one board, because you do not know which board you will get. Twenty-two picks is long enough for the room to empty the running backs, or empty the receivers, or take three quarterbacks and leave both alone. The plan for that turn has to cover a range, written as "if this tier is gone, take that one" rather than as a name.

What you do know is what usually happens. The thirteen highest-ranked running backs on the board all have an ADP inside pick 24 — Jonathan Taylor at 7.5, De'Von Achane at 9.6, Ashton Jeanty at 11.8, James Cook at 13.3, on through Jeremiyah Love at 22.4, plus the two you did not take at pick 1. All but two are three-down bell cows, on the field every down and collecting both carries and catches, which is the scarcest thing in the draft. You take one, and the rest of the position's top disappears while you watch.

Two things this slot cannot buy

Elite tight end. The two best tight ends on the board are Trey McBride, a move TE with 104 catches projected, at ADP 28.9, and Brock Bowers, also a move TE, 103 catches projected, at ADP 35.8. Re-verified against the board on 2026-07-25: both sit just past your 24/25 turn, which sounds like good news and is not. It means the elite tight end is never cheap for you and is available at exactly one turn in the whole draft — and taking him spends half of your best turn on the position with the smallest edge. The best tight end is worth about 5.7 points a week over replacement — the last player at that position you could still start — against 10.7 for the best running back and 8.5 for the best receiver (2026 draft board, replacement baselines). By 48/49 both are gone. Treat the top of the position as out of reach and take a move TE later: the board's tight-end tier one runs seven deep, and Kyle Pitts (move TE, ADP 85.2) and Travis Kelce (move TE, ADP 93.3) are projected to survive to your 72/73 turn.

Elite rushing quarterback. Josh Allen, a rushing QB with 567 rushing yards projected, goes at ADP 28.1 — again just past your turn, and he is the only quarterback the market prices as a real edge. The next four rushing quarterbacks — same archetype, quarterbacks who run by design and so collect a second income stream nobody else at the position has — land in the fifth round and beyond: Lamar Jackson 55.3, Drake Maye 62.3, Jayden Daniels 85.4, Jalen Hurts 93.7. You cannot spend a top-25 pick on a quarterback when that pick is your only shot at a second startable receiver, so this slot is pushed hard toward Late-round quarterback. The compensation: the market is late on the good ones, with Daniels and Hurts going roughly thirty and forty slots after the board ranks them.

The quarterback rule for this slot, stated the same way in Late-round quarterback. No quarterback at 24/25 — that turn is the only place in this draft where two top-twelve receivers are jointly available, and the quarterback edge does not pay for giving that up. Picks 48/49 and 96/97 are the trap: you pay a real pick for a player barely better than free. 72/73 is the one live early window — Jayden Daniels (rushing QB, ADP 85.4) and Jalen Hurts (rushing QB, ADP 93.7) are still on the board there, at a reach of roughly twelve and twenty slots. Otherwise wait to 120/121, where Jaxson Dart (rushing QB, ADP 121.3) lands almost exactly on the pick. Never spend 168, 169 or 192 on a second quarterback: those three are the kicker, the defence, and the IR stash.

Picks 24 and 25 are one decision

Picks 24 and 25 are the last pick of round two and the first of round three — 2.12/3.01 in the round.pick notation the round-by-round section below uses — and at exactly that point the receiver tier is deeper than the running back tier. Read it off Tiers by position: running back tier three holds four players, while seven of the fourteen in receiver tier two still have an ADP past pick 25. Four against seven. Take two receivers and you draw from the deeper pool — and the backs you pass on would have been the fifth and sixth names in a tier already a long step down from what you took at pick 1. That is the compensation for being locked out of the elite tight end and the elite quarterback: 2.12/3.01 is the best place in this draft to take two top-twelve receivers back to back.

Three pairings, in order of preference, names and archetypes read off the board on 2026-07-25.

A — two alpha receivers (the default). Rashee Rice, an alpha X with 96 catches projected, ADP 26.9, and Garrett Wilson, an alpha X with 100 catches projected, ADP 28.3. An alpha is the team's clear first option, targeted regardless of matchup, which makes him the most stable non-running-back thing you can own. Substitutes if one goes early: Zay Flowers, a field-stretching Z — the deep threat, fewer catches but far more yards each, ceiling over floor — ADP 26.5, and DeVonta Smith, a big slot — lines up inside for shorter throws and more of them, which full PPR pays generously — ADP 28.0. Bye clash to avoid — Wilson and Flowers are both bye 13.

B — one running back, one receiver. Only if a back from the position's tier three slides: Josh Jacobs, a three-down bell cow with 282 carries projected, ADP 27.8, or Breece Hall, a three-down bell cow, ADP 29.7. Pair either with the best alpha X left. Safe plus safe, and fine — but see How much to spend on running backs for why a second bell cow is worth less than it feels.

C — safe plus swing. Alpha X first, then variance: Cam Skattebo, a three-down bell cow, ADP 37.6, risk med, or Kyren Williams, a three-down bell cow, ADP 33.1, risk high. Only if pairing A's receivers are gone.

The pairing you never take is a tight end plus anything. It costs you the turn.

The script

Read this on the clock.

Pick 1. Take Bijan Robinson — a three-down bell cow, 280 carries plus 84 catches projected, tied for the top projection on the board at 367 points and flagged low risk. Jahmyr Gibbs is numerically the same player, also 367, also a three-down bell cow, but flagged med risk; if you would rather have the name you know, take him and lose nothing measurable. Fallback if either is somehow off the board: Christian McCaffrey, a three-down bell cow, board rank 3. Do not take a receiver here. Who to take at 1.01 is refreshed last in this build — read it the morning of the draft.

Pick 24. Take the best alpha X still on the board — Rashee Rice first, Garrett Wilson second. If both are gone, take Chris Olave, an alpha X, or Nico Collins, a field-stretching Z, if either has fallen past his ADP of 22.1 or 23.6; if all four are gone, take Josh Jacobs.

Pick 25. Take the second name from the same list. If the receivers have run and a bell cow is sitting there, take the back instead — but no tight end at this turn, and no quarterback either. That turn is the only place in this draft where two top-twelve receivers are jointly available, and the quarterback edge does not pay for giving that up; the full rule is stated above.

The rest, round by round

Keyed to the turn map in 2026 draft board, and to the two-stage payout in How the league is won: weeks 1–14 you need a high mean to finish top six, weeks 15–17 you need a ceiling to win a bracket. So do not sacrifice mean in rounds 1 through 4 — picks 1, 24, 25, 48 and 49 buy the playoff berth — and prefer upside from round 7 onward.

  • 48/49 (4.12/5.01). Best player, still safety-first. The turn map has board rank 36 as the best likely survivor: Bhayshul Tuten, a three-down bell cow, ADP 60.7, which the board ranks 25 slots ahead of the market. Also live: Tyler Warren, a move TE, ADP 53.8, and Drake Maye, a rushing QB, ADP 62.3 — but not Maye, because 48/49 is the first of the two quarterback trap turns. This is where you fill your second running back or your third receiver, not a luxury.
  • 72/73 (6.12/7.01). The tight end and quarterback turn, and the one live early quarterback window — the same call Late-round quarterback makes. Kyle Pitts or Travis Kelce at move TE, and Jayden Daniels (ADP 85.4) or Jalen Hurts (ADP 93.7) at rushing QB, a reach of roughly twelve and twenty slots. Taking one of each here solves two starting slots in one turn at a discount.
  • 96/97 (8.12/9.01). Upside from here — but not a quarterback. This is the second trap turn: Bo Nix, a rushing QB with 362 rushing yards projected, goes at ADP 117.9 and is still there two rounds later. Take backs behind bell cows and receivers with a path to volume.
  • 120/121, 144/145 (rounds 10–13). The quarterback lands here if 72/73 came and went — Jaxson Dart, a rushing QB with 564 rushing yards projected, ADP 121.3, sits almost exactly on the pick. Otherwise swings only. Handcuffs — the backup to a bell cow, worth something only if the starter gets hurt — and rookies. A merely adequate bench player is worth nothing to you: nine of twelve teams in this league lose the same money, so a flat season pays exactly what last place pays.
  • 168/169 and 192. Kicker, defence and the IR stash — by rule and not by value, and never a second quarterback. Do not think about it before you get there — The last two rounds — kicker and defence tells you what to do in about ninety seconds.

Open questions

  • The elite-tight-end claim is a knife edge. McBride at ADP 28.9 and Bowers at 35.8 are just past picks 24 and 25 on a July proxy, so a small ADP shift by late August flips "structurally locked out" into "available, and the pairing logic above has to be reargued". Re-render the board within 48 hours of the draft.
  • ADP here is Fantasy Football Calculator, not ESPN. ESPN rooms are known to push quarterbacks and tight ends earlier than mock-draft aggregates, which would make the lockout stronger, not weaker — but that is a belief, not a measurement.
  • The script assumes twelve human drafters behaving roughly like the market. It has no plan for a room that takes four quarterbacks in round two; Reading the draft room is the note that covers reacting, and none of it applies inside your blind window anyway.
