What basketball teaches you wrong about football

Eleven instincts that make you good at fantasy basketball and will lose you money here, each paired with what football actually does instead.

as of 2026-08-02 · status seed · format espn-ppr1.0-10tm-std

Up: Fantasy Football · Related: Waivers · Opportunity share · Player archetypes · Fantasy football glossary

Why this note exists

Not knowing football is a small problem — you can read a glossary. The real problem is that you already have a working model of a fantasy sport and most of it transfers almost. A wrong transferred model feels like competence: you make a confident decision, for a reason, and the reason is from the wrong game.

So this is a list of false friends. Read the table, then the sections under the ones that will cost you.

The table

Role beats talent, and this is the sentence that matters

In basketball, a star creates his own shot. Give a great scorer the ball and minutes and he manufactures usage out of nothing — that is most of what "great" means.

Football skill players cannot do this. A running back cannot decide to be handed the ball; a receiver cannot decide the quarterback throws to him. Carries (handoffs) and targets (throws in your direction) are allocated by play-calling and by where you line up, and they are allocated before the play starts. A back standing on the sideline when his team reaches the five-yard line scores zero on that drive no matter how good he is.

A mediocre player in a great role beats a great player in a bad role. This is the single most important sentence in this encyclopedia. It is why the twelve Player archetypes describe jobs rather than talent — a blocking TE and a move TE can be equally good at football and one of them is undraftable — and why almost all in-season work is watching for role changes rather than for players improving. See Opportunity share.

Waiver priority: inverted, and the most consequential setting in your league

This is the one that will cost you real money, because the instinct is not merely wrong — it is exactly backwards.

In fantasy basketball, waiver priority or FAAB budget is an asset with genuine option value. You hold it, you wait for the claim worth spending it on, and spending early is undisciplined.

Your league does not use rolling priority. The waiver order resets every week to the inverse of the standings. Three consequences, none of them intuitive:

  1. An unspent claim expires worthless. Saving priority buys nothing — next week's order is recomputed from your record regardless of what you did this week. No carry-over means no option. If a player is worth claiming, claim him; the claim costs zero.
  2. Winning degrades your access. Sit in first and you claim last every week. The better your team gets, the less the wire can help it — which raises the value of your draft, and of being aggressive in the first few weeks before the standings sort.
  3. Losing teams get first crack at every breakout. The system is deliberately compressive: it hands the best available player to the team that needs him most. Once you are winning, you cannot expect to win a contested claim.

One release valve. The acquisition limit is No Limit with a one-day waiver period, so once claims process, everyone unclaimed becomes first-come-first-served. That Wednesday free-for-all is a second, separate market, and it is where a contending team actually competes. Full mechanics in Waivers.

Snaps are not minutes

Snaps are plays a player was on the field for. They look like minutes, so it is tempting to read a snap share the way you read a minutes projection. Do not.

A back can play 60% of his team's snaps and get 8 touches while a teammate plays 40% and gets 18, because which snaps he takes matters enormously — being on the field for passing downs is a different job than being on for first-down runs. Snaps gate opportunity; you cannot score on a play you are not on the field for. But they do not measure it. Touches and targets do.

A target counts on an incompletion

The unit of receiving opportunity is the target, a throw in your direction, not the catch. This has no basketball analogue: there is no stat for a pass intended for you that never arrives.

It matters because targets are far more stable than catches. Catch rate bounces around with the quarterback's accuracy and with luck; target volume reflects the offense's standing decision about who gets the ball, and that persists week to week. In full PPR each catch pays a full point before a yard is gained, so a player earning ten targets a week has a floor a four-target player cannot reach no matter how well he plays.

Efficiency is mostly noise

TS% is a real, sticky trait. A 62% true-shooting player is probably a 62% true-shooting player next year.

Yards per carry is not that. It swings year to year enough to be nearly useless as a forecast, because it depends on the five linemen in front of him, the defences he drew, and a few long runs that may not repeat. A back who averaged 5.1 is not reliably better than one who averaged 4.2. Volume forecasts; efficiency mostly explains what already happened.

Small samples never resolve

Basketball gives you 82 games and several stat lines a week, so luck averages out and one bad decision is 1/82 of a season.

Football gives each player 17 games, and your fantasy regular season uses 14. One bad start/sit call is 1/14 of the campaign that decides whether you qualify, and nothing ever reaches the sample size where you can say a player changed rather than had three odd weeks. So update on role evidence, which is visible immediately, and be slow to update on scoring evidence. See Sample size in a 17-game season.

Touchdowns dominate the variance

A touchdown is six points on a rare event — a player might score six all year. Yards pay 0.1 each, a steady drip. A week's score is therefore a small smooth number plus a large lumpy one.

So a player whose value is mostly touchdowns — a goal-line back is the clean case — has a genuinely wide range of outcomes, and last year's touchdown total is a poor predictor of this year's. Yardage and catches are the signal; touchdowns are the noise you have to survive. See Touchdown variance.

Four that need no section

Streaming for volume is gone. In basketball the trick is scheduling — roster the player with four games this week. Here everyone plays once, or zero on his bye, so there is no volume to harvest. Streaming survives only as a matchup play at kicker and defence: start whoever draws the worst opponent. See Streaming a position.

No categories, so no punt. Your week is one summed number against one opponent's summed number. Everything you know about sacrificing a category has no object to attach to.

Position eligibility is rigid. Nobody is RB/WR. Your only flexibility is the single FLEX slot, which accepts a RB, WR or TE.

Garbage time is a gift. When a team falls far behind, its stars are not pulled — it throws on every down because it has to. Those are real points for the losing team's pass-catchers. A bad team with a good receiver is often a fine fantasy situation, which is the reverse of the instinct that says avoid blowouts.

Open questions

  • The weekly waiver reset is unchanged from the 2026-07-25 settings capture, but the exact Wednesday processing time is unverified.
  • "Efficiency is noise" is directionally right and widely held, but its strength varies by stat: receiving efficiency is somewhat stickier than rushing efficiency, and this note flattens that.
  • Role-over-talent is stated absolutely and is not quite absolute — a few players are good enough to force a role change mid-season. It remains the right default, because you cannot identify them in advance.
