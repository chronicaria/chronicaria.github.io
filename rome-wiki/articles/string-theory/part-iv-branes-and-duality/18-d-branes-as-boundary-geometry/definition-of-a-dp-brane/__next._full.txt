18:T14df8,<h2 id="a-wall-for-open-string-ends">A wall for open-string ends</h2>
<p>Imagine a wall floating in a room. A string can wave around freely, but its two ends are glued to the wall — they slide along the wall's surface and never leave it. That wall is a D-brane. It is not (yet) matter, not a source of force: it is a rule about where strings may end. What makes the module worth writing is that such a rule behaves so much like a physical object that we end up calling it one.</p>
<p>The name encodes the rule. The <strong>D</strong> stands for <strong>Dirichlet</strong>: pinning an endpoint to the wall is a Dirichlet boundary condition on the string coordinates pointing away from the wall (derived in <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/string-theory/part-iv-branes-and-duality/18-d-branes-as-boundary-geometry/boundary-conditions-as-geometry/" class="wiki-link">Boundary conditions as geometry</a>). The <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.625em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal">p</span></span></span></span> counts the wall's spatial dimensions.</p>
