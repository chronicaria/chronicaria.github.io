In string theory there are no vertices to draw, only surfaces — so "more loops" becomes "more handles," and the single knob <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>g</mi><mi>s</mi></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">g_s</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.625em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord"><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0359em;">g</span><span class="msupsub"><span class="vlist-t vlist-t2"><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.1514em;"><span style="top:-2.55em;margin-left:-0.0359em;margin-right:0.05em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:2.7em;"></span><span class="sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight"><span class="mord mathnormal mtight">s</span></span></span></span><span class="vlist-s">​</span></span><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.15em;"><span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span> counts handles. The Feynman expansion is replaced by a <em>topology</em> expansion, and that is the whole content of perturbative string theory.</p>
<h2 id="why-topology-is-the-only-place-a-coupling-can-hide">Why topology is the only place a coupling can hide</h2>
<p>The <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/string-theory/part-v-graduate-bridge/24-interactions-and-riemann-surfaces/splitting-and-joining-worldsheets/" class="wiki-link">worldsheet dynamics</a> is the free <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/string-theory/part-v-graduate-bridge/22-polyakov-action-and-cft/polyakov-action/" class="wiki-link">Polyakov action</a> and nothing else: you cannot bolt a <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>λ</mi><msup><mi>ϕ</mi><mn>3</mn></msup></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\lambda\phi^3</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1.0085em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal">λ</span><span class="mord"><span class="mord mathnormal">ϕ</span><span class="msupsub"><span class="vlist-t"><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.8141em;"><span style="top:-3.063em;margin-right:0.05em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:2.7em;"></span><span class="sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight"><span class="mord mtight">3</span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span> interaction term onto it without wrecking the gauge symmetries. So where does an interaction — and its coupling — come from? The Polyakov path integral already sums over all worldsheet <em>metrics</em> at fixed topology. The one new instruction is:</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Also sum over all worldsheet topologies.</strong> Two-string scattering gets a contribution from the cylinder, the cylinder-with-one-handle, with-two-handles, and so on.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>To weight the topologies you need a number that (a) is invariant under all the symmetries and (b) actually distinguishes topologies. In 2d there is exactly one such term — the Einstein–Hilbert action:
<span class="katex-error" title="ParseError: KaTeX parse error: \tag works only in display equations" style="color:#cc0000">S_{\text{string}} = S_{\text{Poly}} + \lambda\,\chi, \qquad \chi = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int d^2\sigma\,\sqrt{h}\,R. \tag{1}</span>
Here <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0077em;">R</span></span></span></span> is the <strong>worldsheet</strong> Ricci scalar (curvature of the 2d surface, not of spacetime), and we work on the Euclidean worldsheet so the path-integral weight is <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><msup><mi>e</mi><mrow><mo>−</mo><mi>S</mi></mrow></msup></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">e^{-S}</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8413em;"></span><span class="mord"><span class="mord mathnormal">e</span><span class="msupsub"><span class="vlist-t"><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.8413em;"><span style="top:-3.063em;margin-right:0.05em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:2.7em;"></span><span class="sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight"><span class="mord mtight"><span class="mord mtight">−</span><span class="mord mathnormal mtight" style="margin-right:0.0576em;">S</span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span>. In four dimensions this term makes gravity dynamical; in two dimensions the metric is pure gauge, so classically <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>λ</mi><mi>χ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\lambda\chi</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8889em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal">λ</span><span class="mord mathnormal">χ</span></span></span></span> <em>does nothing</em> to the local physics. That is exactly what we want — a term that is invisible locally but sees the global shape.</p>
<p>Its whole job is topological. By <strong>Gauss–Bonnet</strong>, the integral in (1) is a metric-<em>independent</em> integer, the Euler number:
<span class="katex-error" title="ParseError: KaTeX parse error: \tag works only in display equations" style="color:#cc0000">\chi = 2 - 2h \quad(\text{closed, } h \text{ handles}), \qquad \chi = 2 - 2h - b \quad(\text{with } b \text{ boundaries}). \tag{2}</span>

<h2 id="why-a-handle-is-a-loop">Why a handle <em>is</em> a loop</h2>
<p>Think of the two elementary things a string can do: a closed string can <strong>split</strong> into two, and two can <strong>join</strong> into one. Assign the amplitude for one such elementary event a factor of <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>g</mi><mi>s</mi></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">g_s</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.625em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord"><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0359em;">g</span><span class="msupsub"><span class="vlist-t vlist-t2"><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.1514em;"><span style="top:-2.55em;margin-left:-0.0359em;margin-right:0.05em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:2.7em;"></span><span class="sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight"><span class="mord mathnormal mtight">s</span></span></span></span><span class="vlist-s">​</span></span><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.15em;"><span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span>. Now build a one-loop process: a string splits off a virtual closed string, which propagates and is reabsorbed. Geometrically that virtual string is a <em>tube that leaves the surface and comes back</em> — a <strong>handle</strong>. Emit + reabsorb = two elementary events = <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><msubsup><mi>g</mi><mi>s</mi><mn>2</mn></msubsup></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">g_s^2</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1.0611em;vertical-align:-0.247em;"></span><span class="mord"><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0359em;">g</span><span class="msupsub"><span class="vlist-t vlist-t2"><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.8141em;"><span style="top:-2.453em;margin-left:-0.0359em;margin-right:0.05em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:2.7em;"></span><span class="sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight"><span class="mord mathnormal mtight">s</span></span></span><span style="top:-3.063em;margin-right:0.05em;"><span class="pstrut" style="height:2.7em;"></span><span class="sizing reset-size6 size3 mtight"><span class="mord mtight">2</span></span></span></span><span class="vlist-s">​</span></span><span class="vlist-r"><span class="vlist" style="height:0.247em;"><span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span></span>, exactly the cost of one handle in (4).</p>

<h2 id="tree--spheredisk-the-conformal-collapse">Tree = sphere/disk: the conformal collapse</h2>
<p>A tree diagram has external legs running to infinity; conformally those legs collapse to <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/string-theory/part-v-graduate-bridge/24-interactions-and-riemann-surfaces/riemann-surfaces-replace-feynman-graphs/" class="wiki-link">punctures</a> and the surface rounds off to a sphere (closed) or disk (open). So "tree-level four-string scattering" <em>is</em> "the four-punctured sphere/disk," and even at fixed topology the surface still has a <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/string-theory/part-v-graduate-bridge/24-interactions-and-riemann-surfaces/moduli-spaces/" class="wiki-link">moduli space</a> of inequivalent shapes to integrate over — see <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/string-theory/part-v-graduate-bridge/24-interactions-and-riemann-surfaces/the-four-open-string-modulus/" class="wiki-link">The four-open-string modulus</a>. The number of loops is literally the number of handles.</p>
