<p>Almost every confusing UHI claim comes from mixing two things up:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th></th>
<th><strong>Air (2 m) temperature</strong></th>
<th><strong>Surface (skin) temperature</strong></th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>What it is</td>
<td>What a thermometer / your body feels</td>
<td>Roof, pavement, satellite infrared "skin"</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>NYC city-vs-rural</td>
<td>~3–4 °C (<strong>5–7 °F</strong>)</td>
<td>10–15 °C (<strong>18–27 °F</strong>) by day</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Peaks</td>
<td><strong>At night</strong></td>
<td><strong>Midday</strong></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Neighborhood spread</td>
<td>~<strong>2–8 °F</strong></td>
<td>tens of °F (a sunny roof can be <strong>50–90 °F hotter than the air</strong>)</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>The dashboard, your body, and forecasts all care about <strong>air</strong> temperature. When a headline says "20° hotter," it's almost always a satellite <strong>surface</strong> reading. (Sources: US EPA <em>Learn About Heat Islands</em>; NASA/GISS NYC reconnaissance.)</p>
<h2 id="nycs-air-temperature-uhi-the-honest-magnitude">NYC's air-temperature UHI (the honest magnitude)</h2>
<p>The definitive study is <strong>Gedzelman et al. (2003)</strong> (~75-station network):</p>
<ul>
<li>City core averages <strong>+3 °C (5 °F)</strong> warmer than rural on winter/spring nights, <strong>+4 °C (7 °F)</strong> on summer/fall nights.</li>
<li>It's a <strong>nocturnal</strong> phenomenon — near <strong>zero (or slightly negative) at midday</strong>, opening up after sunset because urban materials impede radiative cooling.</li>
<li>On the calmest, clearest, driest nights it reaches <strong>+8 °C+ (14–18 °F)</strong>; wind kills it (JFK: +4.9 °C under &#x3C;5 kt, +2.1 °C over 20 kt).</li>
<li>Coastal quirk: a <strong>sea breeze or backdoor cold front can erase or reverse it</strong> — NYC sometimes runs <em>colder</em> than its western suburbs. NYC's water-on-all-sides siting makes its UHI unusually weather-dependent.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="neighborhood-to-neighborhood-what-you-actually-asked">Neighborhood-to-neighborhood (what you actually asked)</h2>
<p>Realistic <strong>air</strong>-temperature spread between NYC neighborhoods: <strong>~2–5 °F typically, up to ~8 °F</strong> on a hot evening. Measured examples (NYC DOHMH air monitors, 30 Jun 2018, vs LaGuardia 89 °F): <strong>East Harlem +4.9 °F, Bed-Stuy +3.5 °F</strong>; a vegetated Bronx block ~2 °F cooler than a barren Brooklyn one. Rosenzweig calls it a "heat <strong>archipelago</strong>" varying on a <strong>~100 m</strong> scale, not a single dome.</p>
<p><strong>The station quirk worth knowing:</strong> the "official" NYC temperature is <strong>Central Park (KNYC)</strong> — but the park is a partial <strong>cool-island</strong> that caps daytime highs <em>and</em> props up nighttime lows relative to the surrounding pavement, so it *under-*represents the built city. <strong>Newark</strong> is the hottest official site (~29 days/yr ≥90 °F), <strong>JFK</strong> the coolest (coastal). On a clear night the official stations can differ by <strong>several °F</strong>.</p>
<h2 id="what-drives-it">What drives it</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cools:</strong> tree canopy / vegetation (evapotranspiration + shade), waterfront / sea breeze (coastal blocks ~5–9 °F cooler on hot afternoons), high-albedo (reflective) roofs.</li>
<li><strong>Warms:</strong> impervious concrete/asphalt, building density (urban-canyon geometry traps heat, low sky-view factor), anthropogenic heat (AC exhaust, traffic).</li>
<li><strong>Tree shade</strong> is huge on <strong>surface</strong> temp (20–45 °F cooler pavement) but modest on <strong>air</strong> temp: roughly <strong>+10% canopy → ~0.5 °F cooler air</strong>, up to a few °F peak. Concrete "hottest vs coolest neighborhood" maps (e.g. Washington Heights 90% concrete vs waterfront Ferry Point Park 77% vegetation, ~8 °F apart) are <strong>surface</strong> temperature.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="heat-equity-and-redlining">Heat, equity, and redlining</h2>
<p>NYC heat isn't randomly distributed. Formerly <strong>redlined</strong> ('D'-grade) NYC neighborhoods run <strong>~5.8 °F hotter</strong> in land-surface temperature than 'A'-grade ones; the hottest, most heat-vulnerable areas (Heat Vulnerability Index) are the <strong>South Bronx, Northern Manhattan/Harlem, and Central Brooklyn</strong> — lower-income communities of color with less canopy. The <a href="/rome-wiki/wanted/harlem-heat-project/" class="wiki-link wiki-link-missing" title="Unwritten article — a research thread waiting to be followed">Harlem Heat Project +</a> found <em>indoor</em> apartments up to 7 °F hotter than outdoors with heat index >100 °F that never broke overnight. This is why the city's <strong>Cool Neighborhoods NYC</strong> program targets those areas.</p>
<h2 id="feels-like-adds-a-twist">Feels-like adds a twist</h2>
<p>Air temperature is the <em>most</em> spatially variable input; <strong>humidity is smoother</strong> (tracks water/vegetation). So waterfront and park blocks can post a <strong>higher <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/weather/feels-like-temperature/" class="wiki-link">heat index</a></strong> than an inland block at the <em>same</em> air temperature — the muggier air does the splitting. See <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/weather/feels-like-temperature/" class="wiki-link">Feels-like temperature</a>.</p>
<h2 id="for-the-dashboard">For the dashboard</h2>
<p>None of this neighborhood structure is in the forecast <strong>models</strong> — their grid cells are bigger than Manhattan is wide, so Central Park and the South Bronx get essentially the same forecast. Handling that honestly is covered in <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/weather/nyc-weather-dashboard/" class="wiki-link">NYC weather dashboard</a>.</p>
