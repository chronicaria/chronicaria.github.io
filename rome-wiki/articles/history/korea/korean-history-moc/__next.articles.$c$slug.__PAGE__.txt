<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/the-gwageo-examination-system-in-practice/" class="wiki-link">The gwageo examination system in practice</a> — examinations beside protected appointment, education, status, and appointment bottlenecks.</li>

</ul>

<ul>

<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/hangul-as-a-political-and-social-technology/" class="wiki-link">Hangul as a political and social technology</a> — script design, royal statecraft, gendered textual practice, petitions, and later nationalization.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/joseon-land-taxation-from-gwajeon-to-daedong/" class="wiki-link">Joseon land taxation from gwajeon to daedong</a> — official revenue assignments, field assessment, tribute commutation, procurement, and social incidence.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/local-magistrates-clerks-and-yangban-power/" class="wiki-link">Local magistrates, clerks, and yangban power</a> — formal county authority, administrative information, local elite institutions, and unequal access to law.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/markets-and-monetization-in-late-joseon/" class="wiki-link">Markets and monetization in late Joseon</a> — market circuits, fiscal procurement, copper cash, merchant intermediation, and uneven household risk.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/nineteenth-century-tax-disorder-and-peasant-protest/" class="wiki-link">Nineteenth-century tax disorder and peasant protest</a> — how land, military, and grain-account deficits produced unequal burdens and the locally distinct 1862 uprising wave.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/patrilineal-transformation-of-the-joseon-family/" class="wiki-link">Patrilineal transformation of the Joseon family</a> — ritual succession, inheritance, marriage residence, genealogy, and lineage power.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/the-manchu-invasions-and-joseons-place-in-the-qing-order/" class="wiki-link">The Manchu invasions and Joseon's place in the Qing order</a> — the two invasions, coerced hierarchy, retained internal government, and contested memory.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/factional-politics-as-institution-rather-than-pathology/" class="wiki-link">Factional politics as institution rather than pathology</a> — offices, remonstrance, recruitment, kinship, coalition durability, purge, and colonial historiography.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="reform-empire-and-sovereignty">Reform, empire, and sovereignty</h2>
<ul>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/catholic-persecution-and-new-social-networks-in-joseon/" class="wiki-link">Catholic persecution and new social networks in Joseon</a> — books, households, ritual conflict, clandestine organization, successive persecutions, and positioned judicial and martyr archives.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/donghak-thought-before-the-peasant-war/" class="wiki-link">Donghak thought before the peasant war</a> — revelation, ethical cultivation, vernacular practice, clandestine organization, persecution, and the contingent bridge from religion to 1894 mobilization.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/foreign-incursions-before-the-opening-of-korea/" class="wiki-link">Foreign incursions before the opening of Korea</a> — comparing the 1866 French expedition, the 1871 American expedition, Japanese coercion, and why only the last produced a durable treaty regime.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/the-ganghwa-treaty-and-unequal-sovereignty/" class="wiki-link">The Ganghwa Treaty and unequal sovereignty</a> — coercion, nominal independence, treaty-port jurisdiction, coastal surveying, tariff constraints, and Korean responses after 1876.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/gabo-reform-causal-map/" class="wiki-link">Gabo Reform causal map</a> — Korean reform coalitions, Donghak pressure, Japanese military coercion, three reform phases, and uneven social implementation.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/gwangmu-reform-fiscal-and-military-capacity/" class="wiki-link">Gwangmu Reform fiscal and military capacity</a> — revenue growth, fiscal fragmentation, cadastral knowledge, money, armed force, and the sovereignty-capacity distinction.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="colonial-rule-and-mobilization">Colonial rule and mobilization</h2>
<ul>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/colonial-koreas-changing-administrative-regimes/" class="wiki-link">Colonial Korea's changing administrative regimes</a> — military-police rule, constrained cultural politics, developmental administration, and total-war mobilization.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/land-survey-tenancy-and-the-colonial-economy/" class="wiki-link">Land survey, tenancy, and the colonial economy</a> — cadastral title, landlordism, debt, rice policy, agrarian protest, growth, and unequal distribution.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/the-march-first-movement-as-local-and-global-history/" class="wiki-link">The March First Movement as local and global history</a> — religious, student, market-town, and diaspora networks, colonial repression, global claims, and casualty-source limits.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/forced-labor-and-wartime-mobilization/" class="wiki-link">Forced labor and wartime mobilization</a> — staged recruitment, state allocation, formal requisition, corporate discipline, gendered labor, survivor evidence, and contested redress.</li>
<li><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/korea/korean-migration-across-the-japanese-empire/" class="wiki-link">Korean migration across the Japanese empire</a> — household strategies, labor demand, mobility control, racial hierarchy, wartime compulsion, and post-imperial diaspora across Japan, Manchuria, and Sakhalin.</li>
</ul>

</ul>
