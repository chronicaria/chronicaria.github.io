Player archetypes

Twelve labels that describe what a football player does, so that a name you have never heard becomes a thing you can reason about.

Up: Fantasy Football · Next: Fantasy football glossary · Uses: 2026 draft board

Twelve labels that describe what a football player does, so that a name you have never heard becomes a thing you can reason about.

Why this note exists first

You know four NFL players. Everyone else in this draft is a stranger, and a stranger's name tells you nothing. "Bhayshul Tuten, RB21" is not information — it is a label with no content behind it.

So nothing in this encyclopedia ever introduces a player by name alone. Every first reference resolves to one of the twelve archetypes below, and the 2026 draft board's Why column is built from exactly this vocabulary and nothing else. Once you know the twelve, you can read the whole board without knowing a single player.

This is a closed set. If a note uses a thirteenth label, that note is wrong.

Running backs — five labels

A running back's fantasy value is almost entirely how many times he touches the ball, and touches are handed out by the coach, not earned in the moment. Five backs can have identical talent and wildly different value.

Three-down bell cow. Stays on the field on every down, including passing downs. Gets the carries and the catches. This is the most valuable single thing in fantasy football, because it stacks two income streams on one roster spot, and in full PPR the catches are worth a full point each before a yard is gained. Signal on the board: roughly 230+ carries and 30+ catches projected. The legacy Jul. 25 snapshot mechanically labeled 22 backs this way; the promoted August 2 file labels 14, and current status gates can make even that model count more conditional than it looks.

Early-down committee back. Runs on first and second down, comes off the field on third. Real volume, but capped — he cannot score on plays he is not on the field for, and he misses the highest-scoring situations. Signal: carries without catches, or a teammate with a comparable touch count.

Goal-line back. The one they hand it to inside the five-yard line. Touchdowns are six points against 0.1 a yard, so a back with modest yardage and the goal-line role can outscore a back with 400 more yards. But this is the most volatile role in fantasy: touchdowns are rare events, and a player whose value is mostly touchdowns has a wide range of outcomes. See Touchdown variance.

Passing-down back. On the field on third down and in obvious passing situations. Few carries, many catches. Nearly worthless in a league that does not count receptions and genuinely startable in this one — a pure creation of the full-PPR format.

Handcuff. The backup to a bell cow, valuable only if the starter gets hurt. Football's version of a lottery ticket, and a specific kind: because one back typically absorbs almost the whole vacated role rather than the work spreading around, a handcuff can go from unrosterable to a weekly starter in a single Sunday. See The injury-inheritance asymmetry. Insurance backs average roughly 3.2 starts a season, which is why hoarding handcuffs is a floor play, and this league's payout does not reward floor.

Wide receivers — three labels

A receiver scores only when the ball is thrown to him, so the number that matters is targets — throws in his direction, counted whether or not he catches them. Where he lines up largely determines how many he gets.

Alpha X. The team's clear first option. Lines up wide, draws the opponent's best defender, and gets targeted regardless of matchup. High target volume is the most stable thing in fantasy football, more stable than efficiency, so alphas are the safest non-running-back picks on the board. Signal: roughly 85+ catches projected.

Big slot. Lines up inside, closer to the formation. Shorter throws, more of them, fewer yards each. In full PPR this is a much better job than it sounds: ten catches for 80 yards pays 18 points, the same as four catches for 140. Signal: high catch count, modest yards per catch.

Field-stretching Z. The deep threat. Fewer catches, more yards each, and a scoring profile that swings week to week — three catches for 20 yards one week, five for 140 and a touchdown the next. Ceiling over floor, which this league's payout happens to reward. Signal: 14+ yards per catch.

Tight ends — two labels

Tight end is the position where role, not talent, decides everything. Most starting tight ends in the NFL are hired to block. They are on the field constantly and almost never have the ball thrown to them, and they score nothing forever.

Move TE. Split out and used as a receiver. Effectively a large slot receiver who counts at a scarce position, which is where all his value comes from. Signal: roughly 45+ catches projected.

Blocking TE. On the field, blocking. Undraftable in any format, regardless of how good he is at football.

The gap between the two is the whole tight-end story, and it is why the position collapses so fast after the top few names — see Tiers by position.

Quarterbacks — two labels

Every team starts one quarterback and passing is the main way offenses move, so quarterbacks score the most raw points of anyone. That is also why they are often worth less than their raw totals imply: everyone has one, and the refreshed 10-team replacement baseline is the relevant comparison. See Late-round quarterback.

Rushing QB. Runs on purpose, not just when a play breaks down. Rushing yards pay 0.1 a yard and rushing touchdowns pay six against a passing touchdown's four, so a quarterback who runs collects a second income stream nobody else at his position has. These are the only quarterbacks who break the flatness at the top of the position. Signal: 350+ rushing yards projected.

Pocket QB. Throws. Everything he scores comes from passing, where the whole position is compressed. Fine — just take one late.

Kickers and defences

Neither gets an archetype, on purpose. A kicker's value comes from the offense he plays for, not from him; a defence's comes from the matchup. You stream both by Implied team total rather than rostering a name. The last two rounds — kicker and defence covers this, including why this league's scoring makes stock defence rankings unusable.

How to read the board with just this

The Why column on the 2026 draft board is written in a fixed grammar: archetype, then the number that makes the case.

three-down bell cow; 279 carries + 72 catches projected

You do not need to know who that is. You know he is on the field on every down, that he collects both kinds of touches, that the 72 catches are worth 72 points before a yard, and that this is the scarcest thing in the draft. That is the entire point of the label.

Open questions

  • The signal thresholds are cuts on projected stat lines, so a player sitting on a boundary gets a label that is more confident than the underlying projection. The archetype is a summary, not a measurement.
  • "Committee" is inferred from a teammate's projected touch count, which is itself a guess about a coach's plan. Depth-chart resolution at the August 30 cutdown will move several of these labels.
  • There is no archetype for the two-way rookie or the player changing roles mid-season. The set is deliberately closed and will occasionally be a bad fit.
