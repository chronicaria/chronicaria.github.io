The four players you already know

Four names you kept from last year's autodraft, used as four doors into the ideas you actually need before the draft.

Why these four

Gibbs, McBride, Nix and Jones happen to cover four things that decide a fantasy season: what makes a running back scarce, why tight end is a job rather than a talent, why quarterbacks are cheap, and why staying on the field beats being good. Case studies, not scouting reports.

Two kinds of number appear below, and the difference matters. Point totals are projections — nothing that happened. The examples deliberately preserve the archived 2026-07-25 snapshot and its 12-team market proxy to teach the concepts; they are not live draft recommendations. For any pick, use the released August 2 2026 draft board, which replaces those player, ADP, and replacement-level figures with the 10-team model.

Jahmyr Gibbs — what scarcity looks like, and what full PPR does

Gibbs is a three-down bell cow: a back who stays on the field on every down, including passing downs, so he collects both the carries (running plays handed to him) and the catches. See Player archetypes. As of 2026-07-25 players.csv labels 22 players with this archetype, but that is its own mechanical test and it is more than the number of genuinely secure bell-cow jobs: a July projection assumes a settled depth chart, and the August 30 cutdown will demote several of those 22 to committee work. Gibbs sits at the secure end of that list, which is why he is a first-round conversation rather than just a good runner.

The arithmetic, from his row in players.csv as of 2026-07-25: 279 carries and 72 catches projected, totalling 367.4 points across 17 games — the highest projected total of all 243 players in the file, about 21.6 a week.

What he has actually done, observed in player_context.csv: 15 games in 2023, then 17 and 17 — 49 of a possible 51 — with one week listed Out and one Questionable on the NFL's official weekly injury report (hamstring; the designations are defined under Daniel Jones below), and one of the two absences carrying no injury designation at all. He is 24.5 on the September 9 opener. One wrinkle in the label: he took 67.0 percent of his offense's snaps in 2025, eighth among the 65 backs the file has snap counts for. Three-down names the downs he is used on, not a snap monopoly. The board turns that history into Avail 0.92 and Adj VOR +233 — both models, and both move him up rather than down. See Availability and injury risk.

Now the part that matters. Your league is full PPR: one point per reception, before a single yard is gained. So those 72 projected catches are 72 points on their own, roughly 4.2 a game, arriving just for being on the field on passing downs. A back with the same rushing line and no receiving work projects about 72 points lower — the distance between the first pick of the draft and a mid-round one.

Scarcity is a supply fact rather than a talent fact. Your league starts 2 RB plus a flex (a slot taking a running back, receiver or tight end), and the released 10-team model uses RB24 as its replacement baseline. The archived player example illustrates the concept only; see Value over replacement in fantasy football for the current calculation.

Trey McBride — tight end is a usage question, not a talent question

Most starting tight ends in the NFL are hired to block: on the field most of the game, almost never thrown to, scoring nothing forever. That is a blocking TE, undraftable in any format no matter how good he is at football.

McBride is the other kind — a move TE, split out away from the offensive line and targeted like a receiver. A target is a throw in his direction, counted whether or not he catches it.

The whole position is that one distinction. The archived 2026-07-25 snapshot projects McBride for 104 catches and 1,049 yards: the catches pay 104 points, the yards about 105 more at a tenth of a point each, and his projected 250.9 total is mostly those two lines added together. Its former 12-team replacement gap is illustrative only; the current board compares tight ends with TE10.

That projection is a bet not on his talent but on his job continuing — and the job is observable. From player_context.csv, 2025: 169 targets, 126 catches, 27.4 percent of his team's targets, on the field for 91.4 percent of its snaps, the most-targeted of the 32 tight ends in the file. Arizona pays him accordingly: $76 million, $43 million guaranteed, free agency not until 2030 (OverTheCap, retrieved 2026-07-25). And he has been there to collect it — 50 of a possible 51 games across 2023–2025, one week listed Out and two Questionable.

Nobody thinks the last startable tight end is dramatically worse at football. He is worse employed. Evaluating a tight end means evaluating a job description: does his team throw to him, or ask him to block?

Bo Nix — the late-round quarterback argument, in one player

This is the one your basketball instincts will get wrong. Quarterbacks score the most raw points of anyone, because every team starts one and passing is how offenses move. In basketball, the position that scores most is the position you pay for. In football it is the opposite.

In the archived Jul. 25 snapshot he is projected for 298.2 points — 8th among quarterbacks, and 15th among all 243 players in that historical file. Its ADP and old 120/121 turn reference are not usable in the 10-team draft; the August 2 board establishes the current window.

The concept remains that replacement level is high at quarterback: ten teams start one, and the released board measures the gap to the tenth-best. Do not carry forward the archived 12-team/Nix point comparison as a 10-team conclusion.

Two honesty notes. Nix carries the rushing QB label on 362 projected rushing yards, and rushing is the one thing that genuinely separates quarterbacks — rushing yards pay a tenth of a point each, rushing touchdowns six against a passing touchdown's four. And every row in players.csv is projected for exactly 17 games, so the projection file cannot separate a per-game rate from a season total. You may have heard this argument put as "top-8 in total points but only QB10 per game"; I cannot check that historical claim here and am not asserting it. See Late-round quarterback.

Daniel Jones — availability outranks talent

Jones is a pocket QB: everything he scores comes from passing. As of 2026-07-25 players.csv projects him for 253.3 points, 23rd among 32 quarterbacks, and he has no ADP at all — one of 94 players in the file that mock drafters are not taking often enough to produce a number.

Use him for the question that decides more seasons than any ranking: will he be on the field? players.csv cannot answer it — every row there assumes 17 games, a modelling convention rather than a forecast — but player_context.csv records what happened. Observed: 6 games in 2023, 10 in 2024, 13 in 2025 — 29 of a possible 51.

Now the second number, which inverts the obvious conclusion. Across those seasons the official injury report lists him Out in two weeks and Questionable in one, and the only body part it logs for him is a neck. Twenty of his twenty-two missed games carry no injury designation at all — those absences were not medical. A missed game is not automatically an injury: benchings, holdouts and roster moves land in that same column. (It counts weeks he appeared on the report, which is not the same as a medical history.)

The lesson survives and sharpens. The cause does not matter to you — an inactive quarterback scores zero whether the reason was his neck or his coach, and good when he plays is not something you can start.

The NFL publishes a weekly injury report, and its designations are the closest thing to public information you get. Out means he is not playing; doubtful means he almost certainly is not. Questionable is the ambiguous one, and the one that costs you, because it resolves Sunday morning — often after you would ideally have set your lineup. Longer-term, a player on injured reserve is gone for a defined stretch of weeks, and you have exactly one IR slot to park him in. Reading the NFL injury report covers the rest.

Why availability outranks talent: you have 9 starters and 7 bench spots across a 17-week NFL season. A player who misses four games has removed roughly a quarter of what you drafted him for, and covering the hole burns one of those seven spots on a replacement you never wanted. In fantasy basketball, 82 games and daily transactions absorb absences. Here, four missed weeks is nearly a third of the fourteen-week regular season you must finish top six in — and points you do not score because a player was inactive count the same as points he was never good enough to score.

Two labels to keep apart. The board's Avail reads 0.625 for him against a quarterback average of 0.798 — but that is a model: three years of games played shrunk toward the position average, describing what happened rather than predicting 2026. And players.csv reads risk: low for him, which is a projection-confidence flag, not a durability rating. Availability and injury risk is where the method lives.
