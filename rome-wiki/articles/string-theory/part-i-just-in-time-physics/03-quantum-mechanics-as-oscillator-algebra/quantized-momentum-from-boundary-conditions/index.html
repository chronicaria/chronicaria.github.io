    Quantized momentum from boundary conditions

    A free particle can carry any momentum; confine it and only special momenta survive — an interval forces standing waves with q=n\pi/a , a circle forces periodicity and hence p=\hbar n/R with n\in\mathbb Z . That discreteness, chosen by the boundary condition rather than the equation, is the seed of the string's mode sum.

    Waves that must fit

    Think of a guitar string. Left alone, a wave can have any wavelength. Pin down the ends and only the wavelengths that fit — a whole number of half-loops between the pegs — can ring; the rest cancel themselves out. Quantization is nothing more mysterious than this: a wave that has to be consistent with itself can only exist at discrete frequencies.

    Quantum mechanics adds one twist: momentum is wavenumber (times ℏ\hbarℏ), so restricting which waves can exist restricts which momenta a particle can carry. The Schrödinger equation is the same everywhere; what changes from one problem to the next is the shape of the space and the boundary condition it imposes. Two shapes matter for strings:

    • an interval (walls at both ends) — the clamped guitar string,
    • a circle (ends glued together) — a wave chasing its own tail around a loop.

    The equation does not pick the spectrum; the boundary condition does. Same operator, different domain, different allowed momenta.

    The setup: free particle, then a constraint

    Where a particle is free (V=0V=0V=0), the time-independent Schrödinger equation in 1D is

    −ℏ22md2ψdx2=E ψ.-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = E\,\psi .−2mℏ2​dx2d2ψ​=Eψ.

    Its solutions are the plane waves e±iqxe^{\pm iqx}e±iqx (equivalently sin⁡qx,cos⁡qx\sin qx,\cos qxsinqx,cosqx), with

    E = \frac{\hbar^2 q^2}{2m}. \tag{1}

    The energy is fixed once we know the wavenumber qqq; the only real question is which values of qqq the boundary condition allows. That is where the two geometries part ways.

    Case A — particle on a segment (the guitar string)

    Put infinite walls at x=0x=0x=0 and x=ax=ax=a: the potential is 000 inside and ∞\infty∞ outside, so ψ=0\psi=0ψ=0 outside and continuity forces the Dirichlet condition

    ψ(0)=ψ(a)=0.\psi(0)=\psi(a)=0.ψ(0)=ψ(a)=0.

    The wavefunction is pinned to zero at both walls — exactly a string clamped at two pegs. The condition at x=0x=0x=0 kills the cosine; making the surviving sine vanish again at x=ax=ax=a requires qa=nπqa=n\piqa=nπ, so

    ψn(x)=2a sin⁡ ⁣(nπxa),n=1,2,…\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{a}}\,\sin\!\Big(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\Big), \qquad n=1,2,\dotsψn​(x)=a2​​sin(anπx​),n=1,2,…

    The far wall is what discretizes the wavenumber to qn=nπ/aq_n=n\pi/aqn​=nπ/a. The value n=0n=0n=0 is thrown out — it gives ψ≡0\psi\equiv0ψ≡0, which is no state at all. The prefactor normalizes: since ∫0asin⁡2(nπx/a) dx=a/2\int_0^a\sin^2(n\pi x/a)\,dx=a/2∫0a​sin2(nπx/a)dx=a/2, multiplying by 2/a\sqrt{2/a}2/a​ makes ∫0a∣ψn∣2 dx=1\int_0^a|\psi_n|^2\,dx=1∫0a​∣ψn​∣2dx=1. Substituting qnq_nqn​ into (1),

    E_n = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Big(\frac{n\pi}{a}\Big)^2, \qquad n=1,2,\dots \tag{2}

    Only standing waves fitting a whole number of half-wavelengths survive: n λ2=an\,\tfrac{\lambda}{2}=an2λ​=a. Confinement gives a discrete spectrum with a nonzero floor E1>0E_1>0E1​>0: you cannot switch the particle off, because n=0n=0n=0 is forbidden. That floor is a confinement zero-point energy — the same "you can't stand perfectly still" effect as the oscillator's 12ℏω\tfrac12\hbar\omega21​ℏω.

    Case B — particle on a circle (the wave chasing its tail)

    Now change the shape of the space: instead of walls, glue the ends together. Identify y∼y+2πRy\sim y+2\pi Ry∼y+2πR, a circle of circumference 2πR2\pi R2πR. There is no wall, so the wavefunction need not vanish anywhere. The one demand is that yyy and y+2πRy+2\pi Ry+2πR are literally the same point, so the wavefunction must return to the same value after one lap — the periodic condition

    ϕ(y)=ϕ(y+2πR).\phi(y) = \phi(y+2\pi R).ϕ(y)=ϕ(y+2πR).

    This is the decisive difference from the interval. Dirichlet pins ψ\psiψ to zero (sines only). Periodicity pins ϕ\phiϕ to repeat: both sin⁡(ly/R)\sin(ly/R)sin(ly/R) and cos⁡(ly/R)\cos(ly/R)cos(ly/R) survive — periodic exactly when lll is an integer — including the constant. Complex exponentials span the same space and diagonalize momentum directly, so we use those. Try ϕ(y)=eiqy\phi(y)=e^{iqy}ϕ(y)=eiqy; periodicity demands

    eiqy=eiq(y+2πR)  ⟹  e2πiqR=1  ⟹  qR∈Z.e^{iqy} = e^{iq(y+2\pi R)} \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{2\pi i qR}=1 \;\Longrightarrow\; qR \in \mathbb Z.eiqy=eiq(y+2πR)⟹e2πiqR=1⟹qR∈Z.

    So the allowed wavenumbers are

    q=nR,n=0,±1,±2,…q = \frac{n}{R}, \qquad n=0,\pm1,\pm2,\dotsq=Rn​,n=0,±1,±2,…

    (The general rule on any loop of length LLL is q=2πn/Lq=2\pi n/Lq=2πn/L; here L=2πRL=2\pi RL=2πR, so q=n/Rq=n/Rq=n/R.) The normalized eigenfunctions are

    ϕn(y)=12πR einy/R,\phi_n(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi R}}\,e^{iny/R},ϕn​(y)=2πR​1​einy/R,

    with the factor fixed by one trip around, ∫02πR∣ϕn∣2 dy=1\int_0^{2\pi R}|\phi_n|^2\,dy=1∫02πR​∣ϕn​∣2dy=1. They are orthonormal by the standard Fourier computation,

    ∫02πRϕm∗ϕn dy=12πR∫02πRei(n−m)y/R dy=δmn,\int_0^{2\pi R}\phi_m^*\phi_n\,dy=\frac{1}{2\pi R}\int_0^{2\pi R}e^{i(n-m)y/R}\,dy=\delta_{mn},∫02πR​ϕm∗​ϕn​dy=2πR1​∫02πR​ei(n−m)y/Rdy=δmn​,

    because for m≠nm\neq nm=n the exponential completes an integer number of laps and averages to zero, while for m=nm=nm=n it integrates to the circumference.

    Now apply the momentum operator p^=ℏiddy\hat p=\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d}{dy}p^​=iℏ​dyd​:

    \hat p\,\phi_n = \frac{\hbar}{i}\cdot\frac{in}{R}\,\phi_n = \frac{\hbar n}{R}\,\phi_n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;p = \frac{\hbar n}{R},\;\; n\in\mathbb Z\;} \tag{3}

    A plane wave on a circle closes up on itself only if its wavelength divides the circumference an integer number of times, and momentum is just ℏ\hbarℏ times that integer count of wavelengths per lap. The energies follow from (1):

    E_n = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Big(\frac{n}{R}\Big)^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m}. \tag{4}

    Two features separate the circle from the box, and both matter later:

    • n=0n=0n=0 is allowed — the constant mode ϕ0\phi_0ϕ0​, a genuine zero-momentum state. There is no confinement floor on a circle: the ground state sits at E=0E=0E=0. This is exactly why a small extra dimension leaves low-energy physics alone — the constant mode always survives.
    • States pair up as ±n\pm n±n (degenerate, En=E−nE_n=E_{-n}En​=E−n​): the same wave running clockwise or counterclockwise. In the box the two directions superpose into a single standing wave; on the circle they are distinct physical states.

    The spacing ℏ/R\hbar/Rℏ/R is the germ of Kaluza–Klein towers. As R→0R\to0R→0, every n≠0n\neq0n=0 mode has E∼ℏ22m(1/R)2→∞E\sim\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(1/R)^2\to\inftyE∼2mℏ2​(1/R)2→∞, so the excited momentum modes cost too much energy to reach and the small dimension effectively hides.

    Boundary conditions, not the equation, select the spectrum. This exact dichotomy returns for the string: open strings get interval-type endpoint conditions, closed strings get periodicity σ∼σ+2π\sigma\sim\sigma+2\piσ∼σ+2π with left- and right-movers — see A string is many oscillators. And when a target dimension is compact, the closed string's center-of-mass momentum is quantized precisely by (3): the seed of momentum modes, winding, and T-duality.

    Open questions

    • The angle operator θ^\hat\thetaθ^ on the circle is ill-defined (multivalued), so [θ^,p^]=iℏ[\hat\theta,\hat p]=i\hbar[θ^,p^​]=iℏ cannot hold globally; the rigorous variable is eiθe^{i\theta}eiθ. Working directly with p^\hat pp^​ and plane waves sidesteps this and still gives the whole momentum spectrum.
    • The real sin⁡/cos⁡\sin/\cossin/cos basis and the complex e±iny/Re^{\pm iny/R}e±iny/R basis span the same space; the complex one diagonalizes p^\hat pp^​, the real one is manifestly real. For counting states they agree — two real solutions correspond to the pair ±n\pm n±n.

    Exercise

    A particle lives on a circle of circumference LLL (radius R=L/2πR=L/2\piR=L/2π). Suppose you cut the circle open into an interval of the same length LLL with hard walls (Dirichlet), keeping LLL fixed. Compare the ground-state energies of the two systems, and explain in one sentence why they differ.

    Solution

    Circle. From (3)–(4) with R=L/2πR=L/2\piR=L/2π, the allowed momenta are p=ℏn/R=2πℏn/Lp=\hbar n/R = 2\pi\hbar n/Lp=ℏn/R=2πℏn/L. The ground state is n=0n=0n=0: E0circle=0.E_0^{\text{circle}} = 0.E0circle​=0. Interval. From (2) with box width a=La=La=L, the lowest allowed state is n=1n=1n=1: E1interval=ℏ22m(πL)2>0.E_1^{\text{interval}} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Big(\frac{\pi}{L}\Big)^2 > 0.E1interval​=2mℏ2​(Lπ​)2>0. They differ because the boundary conditions differ, even though the equation and the length are identical. The circle admits the constant mode ϕ0=const\phi_0=\text{const}ϕ0​=const — a perfectly periodic, zero-momentum state — so its floor is E=0E=0E=0. The interval forbids the constant mode: a nonzero constant cannot vanish at the walls, so the lowest surviving state must curve up from zero and back down, carrying momentum ∼ℏπ/L\sim\hbar\pi/L∼ℏπ/L and hence a nonzero confinement energy. Gluing the ends removes the walls, and removing the walls removes the zero-point floor.
