The Draft

Late-round quarterback

Only one quarterback starts, and the top of the position is the only real gap in it — buy the very top of the position or buy it for free, and never in between.

as of 2026-07-25 · expires 2026-08-15 · status seed · format espn-ppr1.0-12tm-std

The argument in one move

This league starts one quarterback. Twelve teams, one slot each, and no superflex — a superflex is a second starting slot that also accepts a quarterback, so leagues that have one see most teams start two and the position turn scarce. This league has none (Fantasy football glossary), so exactly twelve quarterbacks start in any given week, out of thirty-two NFL teams. The worst starter in this league is roughly the twelfth-best quarterback in football, and the thirteenth is sitting there free.

That is what a high replacement level means: the quality of player you can get at no cost. Quarterbacks score the most raw points of anyone on the field, and that is irrelevant, because everyone has one. What you are paid for is points above what the position gives away for free — Value over replacement in fantasy football and Replacement level.

Your basketball instincts already own this: it is why a 20-point guard at a deep position is worth less than a 14-point centre at a shallow one.

Replacement is the last player at that position you could still start: the 29th running back and the 31st receiver once the FLEX is accounted for, but only the 12th tight end and the 12th quarterback. Over the 12th-best instead, running back is 6.3 and receiver 5.9; tight end and quarterback are unchanged, because only twelve of each get started.

Quarterback is the flattest position anyone actually starts. The part that matters more is where inside the position that 4.4 lives. From the QB rows of the board (projections are FFToday stat lines dated 2026-07-23, scored under this league's rules):

  • QB1 projects 366 points. QB12 projects 291. That gap is 75 points over a 17-game season — about 4.4 a week.
  • QB2 projects 325. So QB2 through QB12 — eleven quarterbacks, the entire startable middle of the position — are separated by 33 points, about 2.0 a week.

Say it out loud, because it is the whole note: the best quarterback is 2.4 points a week clear of the second-best. The second-best is only 2.0 points a week clear of the twelfth. The top of the position is the only real gap in it. One player holds most of the position's value. Everyone behind him stands on a plateau you can walk onto for nothing in the back half of the draft.

The modern amendment — rushing quarterbacks

The flatness has one real exception, and it is a scoring-rules exception, not an opinion.

In this league, rushing yards pay 0.1 a yard and rushing touchdowns pay six, against a passing touchdown's four. A quarterback who runs on purpose collects a second income stream nobody else at his position has, priced at the same generous rate a running back's yards are. Six hundred rushing yards is 60 points before a single touchdown — about 3.5 a week, roughly the size of the entire QB1-to-QB12 spread, bolted onto a passing line that is already competitive.

This is why the Player archetypes set splits the position in two, and only two:

  • Rushing QB — runs by design, not just when a play collapses. Signal: 350+ rushing yards projected.
  • Pocket QB — throws. Everything he scores comes from the compressed part of the position.

Four quarterbacks on the 2026 board pair elite rushing volume with a genuinely top passing projection. All four carry the rushing QB label:

One caution: the archetype is a label, not a guarantee. Rushing volume alone does not make a quarterback elite — it has to sit on top of real passing production. The board holds a rushing QB projected for 657 rushing yards who still ranks QB19, and another at 494 who ranks QB18. Bo Nix, one of the four names you already know, is a rushing QB at 362 rushing yards and ranks QB8 — squarely on the plateau. The label tells you a player has the second income stream; the projection tells you whether the first one is any good.

What availability adds — and it sharpens the rule rather than changing it

The board no longer treats seventeen games as free. It carries an Avail column — the share of his team's games a player actually played across 2023–2025, shrunk toward his position's average — and an Adj VOR column that rescales each projection by Avail relative to the position mean. Both are models, not observations. See Availability and injury risk.

Quarterback is where they bite hardest. Nine of the twenty largest board-rank moves in that note are quarterbacks, out of a position that supplies eighteen of the board's rows. And the position itself measures worst: the lowest mean availability of the four positions, and near-lost seasons — eight games or fewer — at 16%, the highest of the four and double the receivers'.

That does not overturn this note. It sharpens it. The 4.4-a-week edge you would be buying early is quoted on a seventeen-game assumption, at the position least likely to deliver seventeen games.

What it does change is which quarterback. Of the two names this note endorses at 72/73, Avail splits them hard: Jalen Hurts 0.907, well above the 0.805 quarterback mean, against Jayden Daniels 0.739, well below it — and Availability and injury risk drops Daniels 59 board places once projections are scaled for availability, one of the ten largest falls on the board. Hurts costs about eight more slots of reach and is the one to prefer. Jaxson Dart, the 120/121 name, sits at 0.814, essentially at the position mean, but on a single 14-game season — thin evidence either way.

One thing the adjustment does not show is a rushing-quarterback durability penalty. Across the eighteen quarterbacks on the board, the ten labelled rushing QB average 0.882 availability against 0.848 for the eight pocket QBs — computed from Data/player_context.csv on 2026-07-25. Eighteen players over three seasons is a thin sample, and Avail counts benchings and suspensions alongside injuries, so read that as not visible in this data, not as disproved.

The rule

Stated once here, and the same way in Drafting from the 1.01:

No quarterback at 24/25 — that turn is the only place in this draft where two top-twelve receivers are jointly available, and the quarterback edge does not pay for giving that up. Picks 48/49 and 96/97 are the trap: you pay a real pick for a player barely better than free. 72/73 is the one live early window — Jayden Daniels (rushing QB, ADP 85.4) and Jalen Hurts (rushing QB, ADP 93.7) are still on the board there, at a reach of roughly twelve and twenty slots, and on availability it is Hurts you want. Otherwise wait to 120/121, where Jaxson Dart (rushing QB, ADP 121.3) lands almost exactly on the pick. Never spend 168, 169 or 192 on a second quarterback: those three are the kicker, the defence, and the IR stash.

The middle is the worst place to be for the same structural reason as the running-back dead zone described in How much to spend on running backs: you are paying a real price for a player the position hands out nearly free.

Andrew's turns, labelled

Sixteen picks: 1 · 24 · 25 · 48 · 49 · 72 · 73 · 96 · 97 · 120 · 121 · 144 · 145 · 168 · 169 · 192. Twelve teams, so pick 121 is the start of round 11.

Pick 1 — not a quarterback. The three highest-value players on the board are running backs and the best is worth 10.7 a week over replacement. Spending the 1.01 on a 4.4-a-week edge is the most expensive version of this mistake available.

Picks 24 and 25 — not a quarterback either. QB1's ADP is 28.1, so he is usually still sitting there, and that is exactly the trap. This is the only turn in the draft where two top-twelve receivers are jointly available, and a 4.4-a-week edge does not pay for giving that up. The board also values the top two move tight ends above QB1 at those same picks.

Picks 48, 49 and 96, 97 — traps. Rounds four through nine is where QB2 through QB7 are priced, and every one of them sits on the 2.0-a-week plateau. Paying a fourth- or eighth-round pick to move from QB12 to QB5 buys about one point a week, while the receivers and tight ends at those same picks are worth several times that.

Picks 72 and 73 — the one live early window. Jayden Daniels (rushing QB, 642 projected rushing yards, ADP 85.4) and Jalen Hurts (rushing QB, 456 projected rushing yards, ADP 93.7) are both usually still on the board, at a reach of roughly twelve and twenty slots. That is a genuine rushing profile bought a round or two before the market takes it, and it is the one early price this note endorses. Take Hurts over Daniels if both are there: their projections are five points apart across a whole season, and their Avail is 0.907 against 0.739 — the difference between a quarterback the availability model leaves alone and one it drops 59 board places. Drafting from the 1.01 calls the same turn the tight end and quarterback turn, for the same reason.

Picks 120 and 121, then 144 and 145 — buy here if you did not. Rounds 10 and 11 onward. Jaxson Dart (rushing QB, ADP 121.3) lands almost exactly on the pick, projects 297, and carries 564 projected rushing yards — a genuine second income stream at a price the board rates about 0.3 a week above the twelfth quarterback. That is the trade this note wants: the shape of an elite quarterback's scoring at replacement cost, with every earlier pick kept for positions where the edges are two to three times larger.

Picks 168, 169, 192 — not a quarterback. Those three are the kicker, the defence, and the IR stash — the roster spot that holds an injured player without counting against your sixteen. The two starters are mandatory, so there is no room for a spare arm. A bye week (the one week each NFL team does not play) gets covered off the wire instead, because quarterback is the easiest position to stream — rotate week to week off the wire — since startable ones are always unrostered. League-specific caveat: waiver order resets every week to the inverse of the standings, so a winning team is last in the queue every Wednesday. Streaming still works here because the supply is deep, but do not assume first choice. See Waivers and Streaming a position.

The verdict

Wait. Take one at 72/73 if Hurts or Daniels is there and you are willing to reach twelve to twenty slots — Hurts first, on availability; otherwise plan on 120/121, with 144/145 as the backup. No quarterback at 24/25, none at 48/49 or 96/97, and none at 168, 169 or 192.

Open questions

  • The 4.4-a-week figure is the gap between projected season totals, not an observed result. Quarterback projections compress toward the middle by construction, which may understate how much the top separates in a real season.
  • Durability is now priced, but only backwards. Proj pts still assumes 17 games from every row; Avail and Adj VOR correct for that using three seasons of games played, and both are models of what a player has done. The specific worry this bullet used to carry — that rushing quarterbacks take contact pocket quarterbacks do not — is not visible in the measurement: the board's rushing QBs are the more available group, 0.882 against 0.848. Eighteen quarterbacks over three seasons cannot settle that either way.
  • ADP here is a July proxy for a late-August draft. Quarterback ADP is among the most volatile through preseason, so the 120/121 window is the least stable claim in this note.
