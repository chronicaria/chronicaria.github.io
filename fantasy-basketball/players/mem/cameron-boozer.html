Nine-category: projected top 45-80 per game in a 12-team standard population, with positive FG% volume, points, rebounds and useful assists. Points leagues: stronger than the category band because high-volume points/rebounds/assists and double-double equity outweigh middling stocks and turnovers. Dynasty: Memphis's No. 1 asset and the class's strongest immediate fantasy prospect; a top-20-ish dynasty path is credible, but a precise global rank requires the league-wide synthesis.

Range, strengths and risks

  • Strengths: FG%, scoring, rebounding, out-of-position assists and a playable three-point shot.
  • Liabilities: rookie turnovers, only moderate block translation for a frontcourt player, and FT% that helps less than his high-volume FG%.
  • Floor: Memphis shares creation with Jerome/Grant, NBA length compresses his finishing, and he lands near 15/7/3 in 27 minutes.
  • Base: starts immediately, leads the team in touches and reaches the table's 19.9/8.7/3.6 line.
  • Ceiling: becomes the day-one alpha, sustains near-50% shooting and pushes toward 22/10/4 with 1.8 combined stocks—an early-round outcome.
  • Injury/transaction branches: no current major medical restriction was found. A Grant trade raises usage; Edey absences add small-ball center and rebounding opportunities.

Career record

Pre-NBA: Christopher Columbus High School (Westchester, Florida), then Duke, one season (2025-26). At Columbus he won multiple state championships and took Gatorade National Player of the Year twice, in 2023 as a sophomore and again in 2025, plus Mr. Basketball USA and Florida Mr. Basketball honors. At Duke he started all 38 games and averaged 33.5 minutes, 22.5 points, 10.2 rebounds, 4.1 assists and 1.4 steals on .556 FG / .391 3P / .789 FT, totaling 855 points, 389 rebounds and 157 assists. He led Duke in scoring, rebounding and assists, tied for the national lead with 22 double-doubles, and became the first freshman or sophomore to average 20-10-4 since Larry Bird in 1976-77. Awards: consensus National Player of the Year and the AP award — only the fifth freshman ever to win it — unanimous AP All-America first team, National Freshman of the Year, ACC Player of the Year and ACC Rookie of the Year, and the Karl Malone Award as the top power forward; later named ACC Male Athlete of the Year. Duke went 35-3, won the ACC Tournament and entered the NCAA Tournament as the overall No. 1 seed, then lost 73-72 to UConn in the Elite Eight on a Braylon Mullins buzzer-beater after leading by 15 at half; Boozer had 27 points, eight rebounds, four assists and two blocks in that final college game. His block rate is confirmed at 0.6 per game, which is low for a projected frontcourt anchor and is the one clear hole in an otherwise complete college line. His made-threes per game could not be verified from accessible sources — the 1.4 3PM figure quoted in the player card above is not independently confirmed here and should be treated as unverified, though a .391 mark on a 22.5-point scoring load makes a figure in that range plausible. His father is Carlos Boozer, and his fraternal twin Cayden played point guard at Duke alongside him. Draft: 2026, first round, third overall, Memphis Grizzlies. No draft-night trade.

NBA record: None. Boozer has not played an NBA regular-season game, so there is no career table for him yet. His only professional action is the 2026 Summer League, where across six games in Salt Lake City and Las Vegas he averaged 18.8 points, 6.5 rebounds and 3.5 assists on .554 from the field and .476 from three, and led Memphis to the semifinals, including a game-high 24 points on 10-of-13 shooting with seven rebounds. ESPN's post-Summer-League rankings placed him second in the rookie class behind AJ Dybantsa. Note the sample is six games against mixed competition; the .476 three-point mark in particular should not be projected forward. ESPN, 2026-07

Market: Boozer opened as a co-favorite for 2026-27 Rookie of the Year at roughly a 24% implied probability, level with Darryn Peterson and ahead of No. 1 pick AJ Dybantsa, who lands on a Washington roster with more established veterans. The market's reasoning is opportunity, not a talent verdict, and it matches this dossier's own usage-vacuum argument. SI betting, 2026-07

Fantasy history: None. No NBA season has been played, so no nine-category or points finish exists. The relevant prior is positional: high-usage, high-FG% forwards drafted in the top five who start immediately have historically landed in the top-100 range as rookies, with the category-league drag coming from turnovers and thin blocks rather than from the scoring.

Durability: No NBA games. College availability was perfect — 38 of 38 games started at Duke, and no significant missed time is documented in his high school or college record. That is a clean input, but a one-season college sample carries almost no predictive weight for an 82-game NBA schedule, and rookie forwards have wide games-played variance regardless of prior history.

Trajectory: There is no NBA arc to read, so the useful work is separating what the college line actually establishes from what it only suggests. The 20-10-4 season is genuinely rare and the .556 FG on that volume is the strongest signal in the profile — it means the scoring came from efficient interior work rather than from difficult jumpers, which is the version of a college scorer that translates. The 4.1 assists from a power forward is the second real signal, because passing volume at his position rarely appears and rarely disappears. What the record does not establish is rim protection: Duke's defensive numbers were not driven by his shot-blocking, and the block rate is the single category most likely to disappoint managers who draft him expecting a full frontcourt line. The aging-curve position is trivially favorable at 19, but that cuts both ways for 2026-27 specifically — a 19-year-old rookie is the age cohort most likely to have his minutes managed and his efficiency compressed by NBA length in year one. The honest read is a high floor on role and a wide band on rates.
