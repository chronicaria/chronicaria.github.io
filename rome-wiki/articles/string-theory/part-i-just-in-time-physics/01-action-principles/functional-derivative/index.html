    Functional derivative

    A functional derivative is ordinary calculus done on the space of functions : \delta S/\delta x(t) is one component of the gradient of the action S , and it measures how S responds when you wiggle the path x at the single instant t .

    You already know how to find where a function f(x1,…,xn)f(x_1,\dots,x_n)f(x1​,…,xn​) is flat: set every partial ∂f/∂xi\partial f/\partial x_i∂f/∂xi​ to zero. An action S[x]S[x]S[x] is the same problem with infinitely many variables — one for the height of the path x(t)x(t)x(t) at each instant ttt. The functional derivative δS/δx(t)\delta S/\delta x(t)δS/δx(t) is the partial derivative in the "direction ttt," and demanding that it vanish for every ttt is how physics finds the path a particle actually takes. Every equation of motion in this course is produced this way.

    The object we are differentiating

    The action is a functional: it eats an entire function and returns one number. For a particle,

    S[x]=∫titfL(x(t),x˙(t),t) dt.S[x] = \int_{t_i}^{t_f} L\big(x(t), \dot x(t), t\big)\, dt .S[x]=∫ti​tf​​L(x(t),x˙(t),t)dt.

    The square brackets S[x]S[x]S[x] (not S(x)S(x)S(x), and never S[x(t)]S[x(t)]S[x(t)]) are a deliberate signal: the input is the whole function xxx, not its value at one point. As a mathematician, read this as a map

    S:C1([ti,tf])→R,S : C^1([t_i,t_f]) \to \mathbb{R},S:C1([ti​,tf​])→R,

    on the infinite-dimensional space whose "coordinates" are the values {x(t)}t∈[ti,tf]\{x(t)\}_{t\in[t_i,t_f]}{x(t)}t∈[ti​,tf​]​ — one coordinate per instant.

    The variation is a directional derivative

    Pick a "direction" in path space — any smooth function η(t)\eta(t)η(t) — and a small scalar ϵ\epsilonϵ. The first variation of SSS in that direction is the plain directional (Gâteaux) derivative:

    δS  =  ddϵ S[x+ϵ η]∣ϵ=0.\delta S \;=\; \left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\, S[x + \epsilon\,\eta]\right|_{\epsilon = 0}.δS=dϵd​S[x+ϵη]​ϵ=0​.

    This is the directional derivative you already know; the only novelty is that the "vector" η\etaη is a function. Writing δx(t)≡ϵ η(t)\delta x(t) \equiv \epsilon\,\eta(t)δx(t)≡ϵη(t) for the infinitesimal bump, δS\delta SδS is the piece of S[x+δx]−S[x]S[x+\delta x] - S[x]S[x+δx]−S[x] that is linear in δx\delta xδx. Everything of order (δx)2(\delta x)^2(δx)2 is dropped, because stationarity is a statement about the first-order response — just as ∇f=0\nabla f = 0∇f=0 ignores the curvature of fff.

    From directional derivative to gradient: δS/δx\delta S/\delta xδS/δx

    In finite dimensions the directional derivative packages into a gradient dotted with the direction: df=∑i∂f∂xi dxidf = \sum_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\, dx_idf=∑i​∂xi​∂f​dxi​. Replace the discrete index iii by the continuous "index" ttt, and the sum becomes an integral:

    \delta S \;=\; \int_{t_i}^{t_f} \frac{\delta S}{\delta x(t)}\;\delta x(t)\, dt . \tag{1}

    The kernel δSδx(t)\dfrac{\delta S}{\delta x(t)}δx(t)δS​ is the functional derivative — the continuous analogue of the gradient component ∂f/∂xi\partial f/\partial x_i∂f/∂xi​. Its meaning: it reports the sensitivity of the single number SSS to a bump in the path localized at time ttt. Reading a functional derivative off a computation means massaging δS\delta SδS into the form (1) and picking out whatever multiplies δx(t)\delta x(t)δx(t).

    Sanity via discretization. If the continuum still feels like a leap, slice time into points t1,…,tNt_1,\dots,t_Nt1​,…,tN​. A path becomes a vector (x1,…,xN)(x_1,\dots,x_N)(x1​,…,xN​) and SSS becomes an ordinary function SNS_NSN​, with the familiar

    δSN=∑k=1N∂SN∂xk δxk.\delta S_N=\sum_{k=1}^N \frac{\partial S_N}{\partial x_k}\,\delta x_k.δSN​=∑k=1N​∂xk​∂SN​​δxk​.

    Formula (1) is the N→∞N\to\inftyN→∞ limit: the sum over time-slices becomes an integral and the gradient component ∂SN/∂xk\partial S_N/\partial x_k∂SN​/∂xk​ becomes the kernel δS/δx(t)\delta S/\delta x(t)δS/δx(t). A functional derivative is a gradient with a continuous label — nothing more exotic.

    The building block, the analogue of ∂xi/∂xj=δij\partial x_i/\partial x_j = \delta_{ij}∂xi​/∂xj​=δij​, is the Dirac delta:

    δ x(t)δ x(t′)=δ(t−t′).\frac{\delta\, x(t)}{\delta\, x(t')} = \delta(t - t').δx(t′)δx(t)​=δ(t−t′).

    A bump concentrated near t′t't′ changes only the path-coordinate there, so distinct instants are independent "directions," exactly as ∂xi/∂xj\partial x_i/\partial x_j∂xi​/∂xj​ vanishes for i≠ji\neq ji=j.

    Worked example: read the kernel off the particle action

    Take L=12mx˙2−V(x)L = \tfrac12 m \dot x^2 - V(x)L=21​mx˙2−V(x). Bump x→x+δxx \to x + \delta xx→x+δx and keep first order:

    δS=∫titf(mx˙ ddtδx−V′(x) δx) dt.\delta S = \int_{t_i}^{t_f}\Big( m\dot x\, \frac{d}{dt}\delta x - V'(x)\,\delta x \Big)\, dt .δS=∫ti​tf​​(mx˙dtd​δx−V′(x)δx)dt.

    The first term has the derivative on δx\delta xδx, so it is not yet in the form (1). Integrate by parts to move the derivative off δx\delta xδx:

    δS=[ mx˙ δx ]titf+∫titf(−mx¨−V′(x)) δx dt.\delta S = \big[\, m\dot x\,\delta x\,\big]_{t_i}^{t_f} + \int_{t_i}^{t_f}\big(-m\ddot x - V'(x)\big)\,\delta x\, dt .δS=[mx˙δx]ti​tf​​+∫ti​tf​​(−mx¨−V′(x))δxdt.

    Compare with (1). On the standard variation space with δx(ti)=δx(tf)=0\delta x(t_i)=\delta x(t_f)=0δx(ti​)=δx(tf​)=0 the endpoint term vanishes; if the endpoints are free to move it becomes extra boundary data (see Boundary terms and allowed variations). For fixed endpoints, read off the bulk kernel:

     δSδx(t)=−mx¨(t)−V′(x(t)). \boxed{\ \frac{\delta S}{\delta x(t)} = -m\ddot x(t) - V'(x(t)).\ } δx(t)δS​=−mx¨(t)−V′(x(t)). ​

    The functional gradient of SSS at a path is, pointwise in ttt, exactly minus Newton's equation residual. Requiring the whole gradient to vanish — δS/δx(t)=0\delta S/\delta x(t) = 0δS/δx(t)=0 for all ttt — gives mx¨=−V′(x)m\ddot x = -V'(x)mx¨=−V′(x), Newton's second law. "The particle takes the path where SSS is flat in every direction" and "the particle obeys F=maF=maF=ma" are the same statement. This is the observation that Euler-Lagrange equation promotes into a general recipe.

    For a general first-derivative Lagrangian the same steps give

    S[q]=∫L(q,q˙,t) dt,δSδq(t)=∂L∂q−ddt∂L∂q˙,S[q]=\int L(q,\dot q,t)\,dt,\qquad \frac{\delta S}{\delta q(t)}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q},S[q]=∫L(q,q˙​,t)dt,δq(t)δS​=∂q∂L​−dtd​∂q˙​∂L​,

    again the bulk kernel after the endpoint term is set aside. So the Euler–Lagrange equation is nothing but the infinite-dimensional critical-point condition δS/δq(t)=0\delta S/\delta q(t)=0δS/δq(t)=0.

    One habit worth burning in: do not read off the functional derivative before integrating by parts. Only the coefficient of δx\delta xδx is a gradient component; a coefficient of d(δx)/dtd(\delta x)/dtd(δx)/dt still hides both a bulk piece and a boundary term.

    The fundamental lemma that lets us drop the integral

    Stationarity gives us δS=0\delta S=0δS=0 in every direction, i.e. ∫titfF(t) δx(t) dt=0\int_{t_i}^{t_f} F(t)\,\delta x(t)\, dt = 0∫ti​tf​​F(t)δx(t)dt=0 for every allowed δx\delta xδx, where FFF is the kernel — continuous, for the Lagrangians we meet. Why does this force F(t)≡0F(t)\equiv 0F(t)≡0 rather than merely "FFF integrates against everything to zero"?

    Suppose FFF were nonzero — say F(t0)>0F(t_0)>0F(t0​)>0 at some interior point. By continuity F>0F>0F>0 on a whole neighborhood of t0t_0t0​. Choose a bump δx\delta xδx that is positive there and zero elsewhere; then the integrand is ≥0\geq 0≥0 everywhere and strictly positive on that neighborhood, so the integral is strictly positive — contradicting δS=0\delta S=0δS=0. Hence F(t)=0F(t)=0F(t)=0 at every interior ttt. This is the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations, and it is the rigorous bridge from "flat in every direction" to "the functional derivative vanishes pointwise." The freedom to localize δx\delta xδx is exactly what upgrades one scalar equation (δS=0\delta S=0δS=0) into a differential equation holding at each ttt.

    Open questions

    • What is the honest function space? Physicists are cavalier about C1C^1C1 vs. H1H^1H1 (Sobolev), and about boundary conditions. The clean statement is that the allowed δx\delta xδx form a linear subspace — e.g. functions vanishing at the endpoints — and the functional derivative is only defined relative to that subspace. Connect to Boundary terms and allowed variations.
    • The first variation only locates stationary paths. The second variation δ2S\delta^2 Sδ2S (the Hessian in function space) is what distinguishes a minimum from a saddle. Hamilton's principle asks only for stationarity, which is why physicists say "stationary," not "least." Revisit when we ask whether the relativistic action is a max, min, or saddle.

    Exercise

    Take the free nonrelativistic particle, S[x]=∫titf12mx˙2 dtS[x]=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}\tfrac12 m\dot x^2\,dtS[x]=∫ti​tf​​21​mx˙2dt, and add a term linear in the velocity coupling to a fixed function: L=12mx˙2+A(x) x˙L=\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + A(x)\,\dot xL=21​mx˙2+A(x)x˙, where AAA is a given function of xxx (a one-dimensional "vector potential"). Compute the functional derivative δS/δx(t)\delta S/\delta x(t)δS/δx(t) from scratch, and determine whether the extra term A(x) x˙A(x)\,\dot xA(x)x˙ changes the equation of motion.     Solution

    Bump x→x+δxx\to x+\delta xx→x+δx and keep first order: δS=∫titf(mx˙ ddtδx  +  A(x) ddtδx  +  A′(x) x˙ δx) dt,\delta S = \int_{t_i}^{t_f}\Big( m\dot x\,\tfrac{d}{dt}\delta x \;+\; A(x)\,\tfrac{d}{dt}\delta x \;+\; A'(x)\,\dot x\,\delta x \Big)\,dt,δS=∫ti​tf​​(mx˙dtd​δx+A(x)dtd​δx+A′(x)x˙δx)dt, where the last term comes from varying the xxx inside A(x)A(x)A(x) (chain rule), and the middle term from varying the x˙\dot xx˙.

    Integrate by parts on the two terms carrying ddtδx\tfrac{d}{dt}\delta xdtd​δx: ∫mx˙ ddtδx dt=[ mx˙ δx ]−∫mx¨ δx dt,\int m\dot x\,\tfrac{d}{dt}\delta x\,dt = [\,m\dot x\,\delta x\,] - \int m\ddot x\,\delta x\,dt,∫mx˙dtd​δxdt=[mx˙δx]−∫mx¨δxdt, ∫A(x) ddtδx dt=[ A(x) δx ]−∫ddtA(x) δx dt=[ A δx ]−∫A′(x) x˙ δx dt.\int A(x)\,\tfrac{d}{dt}\delta x\,dt = [\,A(x)\,\delta x\,] - \int \tfrac{d}{dt}A(x)\,\delta x\,dt = [\,A\,\delta x\,] - \int A'(x)\,\dot x\,\delta x\,dt.∫A(x)dtd​δxdt=[A(x)δx]−∫dtd​A(x)δxdt=[Aδx]−∫A′(x)x˙δxdt. Collecting the bulk (endpoint terms vanish for fixed endpoints): δSδx(t)=−mx¨  −  A′(x)x˙  +  A′(x)x˙  =  −mx¨.\frac{\delta S}{\delta x(t)} = -m\ddot x \;-\; A'(x)\dot x \;+\; A'(x)\dot x \;=\; -m\ddot x.δx(t)δS​=−mx¨−A′(x)x˙+A′(x)x˙=−mx¨.     One sentence: in one dimension the term A(x)x˙=ddt ⁣∫xAA(x)\dot x = \tfrac{d}{dt}\!\int^x AA(x)x˙=dtd​∫xA is a total time derivative, so it only shifts SSS by a boundary constant and cannot affect the bulk functional derivative — a first glimpse of why total-derivative (surface) terms are invisible to the equations of motion, though not, as Boundary terms and allowed variations shows, to the boundary physics.
