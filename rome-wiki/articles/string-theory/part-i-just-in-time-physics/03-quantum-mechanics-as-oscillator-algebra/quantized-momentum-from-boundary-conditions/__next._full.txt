1d:T20d4a,<h2 id="waves-that-must-fit">Waves that must fit</h2>
<p>Think of a guitar string. Left alone, a wave can have any wavelength. Pin down the ends and only the wavelengths that fit — a whole number of half-loops between the pegs — can ring; the rest cancel themselves out. Quantization is nothing more mysterious than this: <strong>a wave that has to be consistent with itself can only exist at discrete frequencies.</strong></p>
<p>Quantum mechanics adds one twist: momentum <em>is</em> wavenumber (times <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">ℏ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\hbar</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6889em;"></span><span class="mord">ℏ</span></span></span></span>), so restricting which waves can exist restricts which momenta a particle can carry. The Schrödinger equation is the same everywhere; what changes from one problem to the next is the <em>shape of the space</em> and the boundary condition it imposes. Two shapes matter for strings:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>an interval</strong> (walls at both ends) — the clamped guitar string,</li>
<li><strong>a circle</strong> (ends glued together) — a wave chasing its own tail around a loop.</li>
</ul>
<p>The equation does not pick the spectrum; the boundary condition does. Same operator, different domain, different allowed momenta.</p>
