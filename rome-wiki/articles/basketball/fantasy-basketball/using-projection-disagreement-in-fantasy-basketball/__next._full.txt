<h2 id="what-disagreement-actually-contains">What disagreement actually contains</h2>
<p>Two projections can assign the same player very different values while using almost the same evidence. The gap may come from expected games played, minutes per game, possession rate, role, shooting regression, category conversion, positional eligibility, or the replacement pool. It may also come from something less substantive: different update times, season-total versus per-game displays, rounding, or incompatible league settings.</p>
<p>That makes disagreement a <strong>mixture of signals</strong>. Some dispersion reflects genuine uncertainty about the player's future. Some reflects different but defensible modeling choices. Some is stale information or a data-definition mismatch. Some is duplicated opinion because several public systems share inputs or resemble one another. Treating the raw range as a probability interval confuses all four.</p>
<p>The transferable method is therefore diagnostic:</p>
<ol>
<li>normalize the forecasts to the same target and information cutoff;</li>
<li>decompose their difference into assumptions that have basketball meaning;</li>
<li>assess whether the forecasters are genuinely independent and historically useful;</li>
<li>combine forecasts conservatively rather than choosing the favorite after seeing the answer; and</li>
<li>translate the remaining uncertainty into a format-specific decision against a named alternative.</li>
</ol>
<p>This method applies at drafts, on waivers, and during lineup decisions. It does not produce a universal player ranking. The decision still depends on scoring rules, horizon, replacement level, transaction cost, and roster objective.</p>
<h2 id="first-make-the-forecasts-comparable">First make the forecasts comparable</h2>
<p>Apparent disagreement is often a measurement error. Before interpreting a spread, write a small comparison table with one row per source and at least these fields:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Field</th>
<th>Why it matters</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Information cutoff</td>
<td>One source may include an injury, trade, or rotation change that another has not processed.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Horizon</td>
<td>Rest-of-season totals, per-game rates, next-week totals, and one-game forecasts answer different questions.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Games played</td>
<td>A total can be lower only because the source expects fewer active games.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Minutes per game</td>
<td>Minutes often dominate counting-stat differences.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Statistical target</td>
<td>Raw box score, category z-score, points-league total, and rank are not interchangeable.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>League settings</td>
<td>Categories, points weights, roster depth, eligibility, and games caps change value.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Baseline population</td>
<td>A z-score depends on the chosen mean and standard deviation; surplus depends on replacement.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Update method</td>
<td>A current timestamp does not prove that every underlying assumption was refreshed.</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>For category leagues, retain the primitive box-score projections. A rank difference can hide near-identical forecasts transformed through different category populations. Percentage categories require makes and attempts, not only field-goal or free-throw percentage; <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/basketball/fantasy-basketball/percentage-categories-and-volume/" class="wiki-link">Percentage categories and volume</a> explains why a high-volume shooter has a different marginal effect from a low-volume player at the same percentage. Turnovers may be omitted or inverted by one ranking. In points leagues, apply the league's exact scoring formula to each source's event projection rather than comparing published fantasy-point numbers built for another platform.</p>
<p>The comparison should also freeze a common timestamp. If a player is ruled out at 5:10 p.m., a 4:00 projection and a 5:20 projection are not competing opinions. They occupy different information sets. Archive the values and cutoffs used for a decision so later evaluation does not silently substitute revised forecasts.</p>
<h2 id="decompose-disagreement-into-basketball-assumptions">Decompose disagreement into basketball assumptions</h2>
<p>Once the targets match, locate the assumption that explains the spread. A practical decomposition moves from exposure to rate:</p>
<h3 id="availability-and-games">Availability and games</h3>
<p>One model may assign a high probability of missed games because of an unresolved injury, back-to-back rest risk, or late-season incentive. Another may mechanically use the schedule. This disagreement should be represented as scenarios, not averaged into a mysterious decimal. <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/basketball/fantasy-basketball/how-nba-injury-reports-should-change-fantasy-decisions/" class="wiki-link">How NBA injury reports should change fantasy decisions</a> and <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/basketball/fantasy-basketball/late-season-shutdown-and-rest-risk-in-fantasy-basketball/" class="wiki-link">Late-season shutdown and rest risk in fantasy basketball</a> distinguish official evidence from inference.</p>
<h3 id="minutes-and-rotation">Minutes and rotation</h3>
<p>Minutes are not merely a trend line. They arise from starting status, substitution pattern, lineup fit, foul risk, blowout exposure, and the availability of teammates. Consult the evidence sequence in <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/basketball/fantasy-basketball/fantasy-basketball-role-change-checklist/" class="wiki-link">Fantasy basketball role-change checklist</a>: official status, starts, rotation placement, competitive-game minutes, closing groups, and the cause of the opening. NBA Stats supplies official traditional and tracking data, but its fields remain observations rather than guarantees that a coach will preserve the role.</p>
<h3 id="per-minute-production-and-usage">Per-minute production and usage</h3>
<p>If minutes agree but points or assists do not, inspect attempts, touches, potential assists, usage, and teammate context. NBA's glossary defines measures such as pace and tracking fields, making it possible to state exactly which observed quantity supports an assumption. A model may regress a hot shooting stretch sharply toward a longer baseline; another may infer that a new on-ball role changes the relevant baseline. The correct question is not “which number looks better?” but “what persistent mechanism would make the newer rate more representative?”</p>
<h3 id="schedule-and-scoring-period-exposure">Schedule and scoring-period exposure</h3>
<p>Rest-of-season systems may agree on per-game skill but differ in remaining games or expected active dates. For weekly decisions, usable games depend on lineup locks and already-full roster days, not just the NBA schedule. <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/basketball/fantasy-basketball/games-played-versus-per-game-value-in-fantasy-basketball/" class="wiki-link">Games played versus per-game value in fantasy basketball</a> and <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/basketball/fantasy-basketball/fantasy-basketball-streaming-on-low-volume-days/" class="wiki-link">Fantasy basketball streaming on low-volume days</a> show why a nominal fourth game can have zero marginal value if the lineup slot is unavailable.</p>
<h3 id="category-transformation-and-replacement">Category transformation and replacement</h3>
<p>Identical box-score lines can yield different ranks because the systems use different player pools, standard deviations, positional adjustments, or scarcity rules. Recompute value against the league's actual feasible alternative. <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/basketball/fantasy-basketball/fantasy-basketball-replacement-level/" class="wiki-link">Fantasy basketball replacement level</a> is the essential bridge: the relevant quantity is not a player's isolated score but the improvement over the player or empty slot that would otherwise be used.</p>
<p>After this decomposition, label every difference as one of four types: <strong>information</strong>, <strong>structural assumption</strong>, <strong>transformation</strong>, or <strong>unexplained residual</strong>. The residual deserves caution; it is not evidence that an average is automatically correct.</p>
<h2 id="disagreement-is-not-a-calibrated-uncertainty-interval">Disagreement is not a calibrated uncertainty interval</h2>
<p>Suppose three systems project 28, 31, and 37 fantasy points per game. The range 28–37 does not mean the outcome has a stated probability of falling inside that interval. The systems might share the same historical feed, injury assumptions, and regression approach. Their errors may be highly correlated. They might all omit a possible rotation change. Conversely, a single model may issue a wide, well-calibrated predictive distribution even when all point forecasts cluster near 31.</p>
<p>Forecaster dispersion measures <strong>between-model variation</strong>. Outcome uncertainty also includes within-model uncertainty: shooting, steals and blocks, foul trouble, overtime, injury, coaching decisions, and opponent context. A useful predictive variance can be conceptualized as</p>

In ordinary fantasy use it is a reminder: the spread among point estimates captures only one component.</p>
<p>Nor does agreement prove safety. Highly similar systems can share a blind spot. Consensus can be narrow immediately before a surprise lineup change because every source used the same depth chart. Agreement should raise confidence only to the extent that the inputs, assumptions, and errors are diverse and historically calibrated.</p>
<h2 id="build-a-conservative-consensus">Build a conservative consensus</h2>
<p>Forecast-combination research supplies a useful baseline: combining imperfect forecasts can improve robustness, while elaborate estimated weights can fail when histories are short or the environment changes. The Federal Reserve's Clark and McCracken study compares simple averages, medians, trimmed means, regressions, Bayesian model averaging, and other combinations under uncertain instability. This is not basketball-specific validation. It supports a modest procedure when no fantasy model has demonstrated stable dominance.</p>
<p>Start with the <strong>median</strong> of normalized forecasts. It is transparent and resistant to one extreme value. A trimmed mean is reasonable with a larger panel. Do not weight a familiar brand more heavily by intuition alone.</p>
<p>Historical weighting is justified only with a clean archive of forecasts frozen before outcomes. Evaluate the target that matters: games, minutes, each category, or formula-specific fantasy points. Separate preseason, in-season stable-role, injury-return, rookie, and trade-transition regimes when sample size permits. A system that excels on established starters may be poor at temporary roles.</p>
<p>Weights should be based on out-of-sample performance and shrunk toward equal weighting. If inverse-error weighting is used, cap weights so a small hot sample cannot make one system sovereign. Correlated systems should not receive three independent votes merely because they appear under three labels. Estimate error correlations where the archive allows; otherwise document likely shared lineage and keep the ensemble simple.</p>
<p>Use proper scoring rules when sources provide distributions or probabilities. Gneiting and Raftery define proper scoring rules as those that reward honest probabilistic forecasts; the continuous ranked probability score can assess an entire predictive distribution. Point-error metrics alone can favor a model that gives useful means but dangerously narrow intervals. <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/basketball/fantasy-basketball/fantasy-basketball-decision-calibration/" class="wiki-link">Fantasy basketball decision calibration</a> explains reliability, sharpness, Brier scores for events, and the need to compare like populations.</p>
<p>Most importantly, set the combination rule <strong>before</strong> observing which forecast was closest. Retrospectively selecting the winning model is not evaluation. It is outcome-driven overfitting.</p>
<h2 id="turn-the-spread-into-scenarios">Turn the spread into scenarios</h2>
<p>Disagreement becomes actionable when each important forecast maps to a causal state. Consider a hypothetical guard whose normalized projections imply 26, 31, and 36 fantasy points per game.</p>
<ul>
<li>The low case assumes 25 minutes after an injured starter returns.</li>
<li>The middle case assumes 29 minutes in a sixth-man role.</li>
<li>The high case assumes 33 minutes and continued primary creation because the return is delayed.</li>
</ul>
<p>Instead of averaging blindly to 31, assign explicit probabilities to the role states using current evidence. Within each state, retain an outcome distribution. Then compute value against the alternative:</p>

<p>This framework exposes reversal conditions. If the candidate is superior only in the delayed-return state, the decision hinges on that probability. If the candidate beats replacement even in the low case, model disagreement matters less. If the mean edge disappears after one crowded lineup day, schedule usability—not projection accuracy—decides the move.</p>
<h2 id="a-repeatable-disagreement-worksheet">A repeatable disagreement worksheet</h2>
<h3 id="1-define-the-decision">1. Define the decision</h3>
<p>Name the horizon, format, candidate, feasible alternative, deadline, and cost. “Is this player good?” is not a decision. “Which of two eligible players provides more expected category-win probability through Sunday, given three usable lineup dates?” is.</p>
<h3 id="2-freeze-and-normalize">2. Freeze and normalize</h3>
<p>Record each projection, timestamp, horizon, expected games, minutes, rates, and scoring transformation. Reject comparisons that cannot be reconciled.</p>
<h3 id="3-audit-independence">3. Audit independence</h3>
<p>List shared data feeds, public injury news, likely projection lineage, and update behavior. Do not pretend the number of websites equals the number of independent models.</p>
<h3 id="4-explain-the-dispersion">4. Explain the dispersion</h3>
<p>Attribute as much of the difference as possible to availability, minutes, rate, schedule, or valuation. Flag what remains unexplained.</p>
<h3 id="5-form-the-baseline">5. Form the baseline</h3>
<p>Use a median or preregistered weighted ensemble. Preserve individual forecasts; do not erase the reasons they differ.</p>
<h3 id="6-create-role-scenarios">6. Create role scenarios</h3>
<p>Build low, middle, and high states from basketball mechanisms. Attach probabilities as estimates, clearly distinguished from official facts.</p>
<h3 id="7-compare-full-distributions">7. Compare full distributions</h3>
<p>Include downside and upside relative to replacement. For category play, account for percentages, turnovers, and <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/basketball/fantasy-basketball/category-covariance-in-fantasy-basketball-roster-construction/" class="wiki-link">category covariance</a> rather than adding nine independent averages.</p>
<h3 id="8-apply-a-decision-threshold">8. Apply a decision threshold</h3>
<p>Act only when the expected benefit clears costs and the conclusion survives reasonable probability changes. Close calls should often resolve to preserving flexibility, not forced churn.</p>
<h3 id="9-log-reversal-conditions">9. Log reversal conditions</h3>
<p>State what new evidence would change the choice: a player ruled active, a second straight closing stint, a minutes cap, or a newly congested schedule day.</p>
<h3 id="10-score-later-without-hindsight">10. Score later without hindsight</h3>
<p>Compare the frozen ensemble and components with outcomes across many comparable cases. Separate projection error, information surprise, and utility-model error. One correct outlier is not proof of superiority; one consensus miss is not proof that averaging is useless.</p>
<h2 id="common-failure-modes">Common failure modes</h2>
<p><strong>Taking the highest forecast because it offers upside.</strong> Upside belongs in a distribution, not in whichever source happens to print the largest mean.</p>
<p><strong>Calling the full range a confidence interval.</strong> A collection of point forecasts has no coverage guarantee.</p>
<p><strong>Counting correlated sources as independent confirmation.</strong> Shared inputs and public news create common errors.</p>
<p><strong>Comparing ranks rather than primitives.</strong> Rank embeds format, population, replacement, and category transformations.</p>
<p><strong>Weighting by reputation without an archive.</strong> Familiarity is not out-of-sample evidence.</p>
<p><strong>Learning weights on revised projections.</strong> Post-event updates contaminate evaluation; freeze the information cutoff.</p>
<p><strong>Overfitting rare role changes.</strong> Injury replacements, rookies, and trade arrivals are precisely where historical samples are smallest and structural breaks largest.</p>
<p><strong>Ignoring the no-move alternative.</strong> A projection edge can be smaller than the cost of the drop, waiver priority, or lost roster flexibility.</p>
<h2 id="why-it-matters">Why it matters</h2>
<p>Projection disagreement is valuable because it directs attention to the fragile link in a decision. A wide spread may reveal that minutes, not talent, need research. A rank conflict may reveal incompatible category baselines rather than substantive disagreement. A lone optimistic model may identify a genuine role scenario—or merely stale weighting. The method makes those possibilities testable.</p>
<p>The deepest benefit is epistemic discipline. Consensus is a baseline, not an oracle; an outlier is a hypothesis, not privileged insight. Normalize first, explain the assumptions, combine conservatively, preserve within-model uncertainty, and compare the resulting distribution with a real alternative. That process cannot eliminate fantasy-basketball variance. It can prevent avoidable errors caused by treating several opaque numbers as more informative than they are.</p>
