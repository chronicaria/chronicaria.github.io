Reading an NFL depth chart

An ordered list of who plays each position for a team — useful, frequently dishonest, and the source of the single worst naming collision in fantasy football.

What the thing actually is

A depth chart is a team's ordered list of players at each position: first-string, second-string, third-string. Quarterback has one line, running back has one, wide receiver has three or four, tight end has two. It is the roster sorted by who the coaches expect to play most. See Fantasy football glossary.

Everyone quotes it because it is the only public, structured statement of who is ahead of whom. That is also its problem. The ordering is a label, and what you actually want is a quantity: how much of the offense goes through this player.

The two quantities that matter:

  • Snap share — the percentage of his team's offensive plays a player is on the field for. A snap is one play. A player on the field for 90% of snaps has roughly three times the chance to score as one at 30%, before anything else is true about him.
  • Target share — the percentage of his team's throws aimed at him. A target is a pass thrown in his direction, counted whether or not he catches it. In full PPR each catch is worth a point before a yard is gained, so for receivers and tight ends this is nearly the whole story. Opportunity share is the note that treats this properly.

The depth chart is a rough proxy for both, and only a rough one. A team's first-string tight end can be a blocking TE who is on the field constantly and gets thrown to four times a month — top of the depth chart, worth nothing. A team's third receiver can be a big slot who plays 85% of snaps and eats short targets all day. The order is right; the value ordering it implies is wrong. The archetypes in Player archetypes exist precisely to describe the job rather than the rank.

The WR1 collision — the thing that will confuse you first

"WR1" means two unrelated things, and both are in constant use, often in the same sentence.

Team WR1 = the best receiver on his own roster. Depth-chart language. There are 32 of them, one per team, and being one says nothing about quality — the worst offense in the league has a WR1.

Fantasy WR1 = a top-10 receiver league-wide in this 10-team league. Ranking language. Being one is a claim about a player's absolute value.

So:

"He's their WR1" — he is the best receiver on a particular team. Could be the 40th-best receiver in fantasy.

"He's a WR1" — he is a top-12 receiver in the sport. Almost certainly also his own team's best, but that is a side effect, not the claim.

Possessive means team rank. An article — a WR1, a low-end WR2 — means fantasy rank. That grammatical tell is reliable enough to lean on, and when it is ambiguous the repair is one question: out of how many? Out of his team, or out of everybody?

The same overload runs through every position:

Term Team meaning Fantasy meaning
RB1 Lead back on his own team Top-12 running back league-wide
WR1 Top receiver on his own team Top-12 receiver league-wide
TE1 Starting tight end on his own team Top-12 tight end league-wide
QB1 Starting quarterback Top-12 quarterback league-wide

Tight end is where this does the most damage, because there the two meanings barely overlap. All 32 teams have a TE1. Roughly six of them are move TEs who are actually targeted; the rest are blockers. "Their TE1" is close to meaningless as a fantasy statement, while "a TE1" is a strong one.

Quarterback runs the opposite way and is almost a non-statement in both directions: every team's QB1 starts, and "a QB1" barely narrows anything. The rendered 10-team board now establishes the current top-to-tenth gap; the Jul. 25 12-team point claims remain historical only.

Two more usages you will meet, plus one caution that applies to both:

  • WR2 / WR3 as fantasy tiers — ranks 11–20 and 21–30 for this 10-team league. These are league tiers, not team depth-chart labels.
  • "WR1 overall" — the single best receiver in fantasy. One player, not twelve.
  • Those blocks of ten are not this league's lineup. We start 2 WR plus a flex, so the rendered 10-team replacement model uses WR26, not a top-20 cutoff. "WR3" here can still be a real starter, not a spare part.

A basketball false friend. In fantasy basketball, depth-chart position and minutes are close to the same fact, and a starting job is a strong signal. In football they come apart. A player can "start" and be off the field for half the offense's plays, because football substitutes constantly by situation — different personnel on first down, on third down, near the goal line. Football's real equivalent of minutes is snap share, and the depth chart reports rank, not minutes. Also, basketball's positional labels do not carry this double meaning; the football habit of using one token for both team order and league-wide tier has no NBA counterpart, so there is no instinct to import here. More in What basketball teaches you wrong about football.

Official depth charts lie

Every NFL team publishes an official depth chart. Treat it as a press release, not data.

They are unreliable in specific, predictable ways:

  • Politics. A team that spent a high draft pick or a large contract on a player lists him first whether or not he is playing first, because demoting him publicly is a story the coach does not want.
  • Alphabetical or "OR" listings. Teams routinely list two players as co-starters separated by "OR", or list a group alphabetically, precisely to say nothing.
  • Veteran seniority. Ordering by service time is common in preseason and is corrected on the field in September, not on the chart.
  • They lag. Charts get updated late, if at all, and rarely reflect a role change already visible in snap counts.

There is no penalty for a team publishing a misleading depth chart, and there are competitive reasons to do it. So the incentive runs one way.

Use a fantasy depth chart instead. Fantasy sites build their own, ordering players by expected snaps, touches and targets rather than by team-declared rank, and they revise them weekly against actual usage. Where the two disagree, the fantasy version is the one that has been checked against what happened. Where fantasy football information comes from covers which of these are worth reading. The 32 offenses is this vault's own version — the per-team view of who is actually getting the ball.

The reliable move: whenever you see a depth-chart claim, convert it into the usage claim it is standing in for. "He's the WR2" tells you nothing you can score. "He's on the field for about three-quarters of the snaps and gets a fifth of the targets" tells you almost everything.
