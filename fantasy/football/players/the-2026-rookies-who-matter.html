The 2026 rookies who matter

Why a football rookie is different

In basketball, a rookie earns minutes over two or three seasons, and a first-year player who matters in fantasy is a rare event. In football the job is assigned, not earned. Roles are handed out by a coaching staff before week 1, and a team that spends a high pick on a running back has publicly committed to using him — the pick is the plan, and the plan is usually executed immediately. A back drafted third overall can carry the ball twenty times in his first professional game.

So your basketball instinct here is a false friend: "wait a year on rookies" is correct in one sport and expensive in the other. The reason this note exists is that you have no prior on any of these names, and your leaguemates who watched college football do. Draft capital dominance is the concept underneath it — where a player was drafted predicts his workload better than anything he does on the field, because the team that spent the pick has an incentive to justify it.

How I established rookie status

I did not work from memory. I pulled the full 2026 NFL draft results (all 257 picks, rounds 1–7) from published draft trackers, then intersected that list against Data/players.csv and kept only players who appear in both. Anyone I could not match to a confirmed 2026 draft pick is not in this note, including undrafted rookies who may well make rosters — the file gives no rookie flag, so an undrafted first-year player is invisible to this method. I have not invented a college statistic, a draft position, or a combine number anywhere below.

Two labels apply to every line that follows. ADP is average draft position — the average slot a player goes at across thousands of practice drafts, i.e. his market price (see ADP as a market price and the Fantasy football glossary). The figures here are the Fantasy Football Calculator PPR 12-team proxy, not ESPN's own ADP, which could not be extracted. And every points total is a projection built from FFToday stat lines dated 2026-07-23, scored under this league's rules. It is a forecast. Nothing below has happened.

Archetype strings are quoted verbatim from the archetype column — the closed set defined in Player archetypes.

The backs

Jeremiyah Love (Arizona, drafted 3rd overall). three-down bell cow. The projection has him on the field on every down, collecting 246 carries and 56 catches — two income streams on one roster spot, which is the most valuable single shape in this format. Proxy ADP 22.4, projected 259.9 points. He is the only rookie priced as a genuine early-round asset, and at that price he will be gone before your 24–25 turn.

Jadarian Price (Seattle, drafted 32nd overall). early-down committee back. The projection gives him real volume but caps it — 195 carries against only 31 catches, which means he comes off the field on passing downs and misses the highest-scoring situations. Proxy ADP 72.1, projected 185.3 points, risk flag high.

Mike Washington Jr. (Las Vegas, drafted 122nd overall). handcuff — the backup whose value is almost entirely the chance the starter gets hurt. 85 carries projected, 57.6 projected points, and no proxy ADP at all, meaning he is going undrafted in most rooms. Not a pick; a name to know if Las Vegas's starter goes down.

The receivers

Six of the eleven are wideouts, which is what happens when a draft class is thin at running back and deep at receiver. Note that five of the six carry the same archetype:

Carnell Tate (Tennessee, 4th overall). big slot — lines up inside, catches a lot of short throws. In full PPR that job is much better than it sounds, because each catch is a full point before a yard. 65 catches for 867 yards projected. Proxy ADP 62.3, projected 181.7 points.

Jordyn Tyson (New Orleans, 8th overall). big slot. 61 catches for 783 yards projected — a first-option volume profile on a team the projection does not expect to score much. Proxy ADP 82.7, projected 163.3 points.

KC Concepcion (Cleveland, 24th overall). big slot. 58 catches for 752 yards projected, and the only rookie receiver here carrying a low risk flag. Proxy ADP 119.9, projected 153.6 points. That combination — the best projection-to-price ratio of the rookie receivers — is the one to actually notice.

Makai Lemon (Philadelphia, 20th overall). big slot. 47 catches for 558 yards projected: a real role, third or fourth in line in a crowded offense. Proxy ADP 104.1, projected 120.8 points.

Omar Cooper Jr. (New York Jets, 30th overall). big slot. 37 catches for 466 yards projected. Proxy ADP 132.9, projected 107.6 points.

Denzel Boston (Cleveland, 39th overall). big slot. 40 catches for 526 yards projected. Proxy ADP 132.9, projected 104.6 points — and note he shares an offense with Concepcion above, so the two of them are competing for the same Opportunity share.

The quarterback and the kicker

Fernando Mendoza (Las Vegas, 1st overall). pocket QB — everything he scores comes from throwing, which is the flattest, least valuable shape at the least valuable position. 186.9 projected points — 30th of the 32 quarterbacks in the file, and about 105 points below the twelfth-best quarterback's 291.4, which is the line that matters in a league where all twelve teams start exactly one. No proxy ADP. The first pick in the NFL draft is not a fantasy asset. Do not let the "1st overall" tag do any work here.

Trey Smack (Green Bay, 216th overall). The only kicker drafted in 2026. 142.0 projected points, which is a modelled figure — kicker totals in this file are built from a field-goal distance mix, not from a projected stat line. No proxy ADP. Kickers are streamed by Implied team total, never rostered by name, and a rookie kicker fighting two veterans for the job is the least resolvable situation on the board.

The only August instruction: ignore preseason box scores

Preseason games are played in August and the statistics from them are actively misleading. Starters play one or two possessions and then sit; the leaders in preseason rushing yards are, almost every year, players who will be cut before week 1. A rookie running up 90 yards against fourth-string defenders has told you nothing.

Two things from August do matter, and neither is a box score:

  1. Depth-chart position — where the coaching staff lists him, and who is ahead of him. Reading an NFL depth chart covers how to read one without being fooled by it.
  2. Injury news — specifically injuries to the player ahead of him, which is how most rookies get promoted.

And one date dominates: the August 30 roster cutdown, when every team must reduce to its final roster. That is the largest single depth-chart resolution of the year, it happens in a day, and it reprices dozens of the players above — several of the high risk flags in this note exist precisely because the file was built before it. If your league draft falls after August 30, this note should be rebuilt first.

Open questions

  • Confidence in the identifications is high but not complete. All eleven are confirmed against published 2026 draft results and matched by name to the CSV, so I am confident none of these is a mislabelled veteran. The gap runs the other way: undrafted 2026 rookies are undetectable by this method, so the list may be short by one or two — most likely a receiver or back on a thin depth chart.
  • Name matching is exact-string, so a rookie entered in the file under a different spelling or a suffix difference would have been silently dropped.
  • Six of eleven carry the identical big slot label, which is more likely a limitation of applying a closed archetype set to players with no professional snaps than a real fact about six different offenses.
