    Fixed versus free endpoints

    A string's two ends can each be nailed down (Dirichlet, y=0 ) or left to slide (Neumann, \partial_x y=0 ). The choice leaves the nonzero frequencies identical but swaps sine profiles for cosine, adds a drifting zero mode for free ends, and decides whether transverse momentum is conserved.

    Two ways to end a string

    A vibrating string on 0≤x≤a0\le x\le a0≤x≤a obeys the wave equation in the bulk, but the wave equation alone doesn't know what happens at the two ends. You have to tell it. There are exactly two natural things to fix at an endpoint:

    • the height yyy — pin the end to a wall. This is Dirichlet.
    • the slope ∂xy\partial_x y∂x​y — let the end ride a frictionless massless ring on a vertical pole, so the string always meets the pole horizontally. This is Neumann.

    The gut-level difference: a nailed end has a wall pushing on it to hold it in place, while a free end feels no transverse force at all. That single distinction — who is allowed to push on the endpoint — is what generates every downstream consequence below, and it is the seed of the D-brane.

    Why exactly these two: the endpoint comes from the ring

    The Neumann condition isn't an arbitrary rule; it's forced by Newton. Picture the massless ring threaded on the pole. The pole blocks horizontal motion but exerts no vertical friction, so the only vertical force on the ring is the string pulling on it — the transverse component of the tension, T0 ∂xyT_0\,\partial_x yT0​∂x​y. A massless object with any net force on it would have infinite acceleration, which is impossible. Hence the force must vanish: ∂xy(t,0)=∂xy(t,a)=0.\partial_x y(t,0)=\partial_x y(t,a)=0.∂x​y(t,0)=∂x​y(t,a)=0. The end is free to move in yyy, but the string always leaves the pole flat. Dirichlet is the opposite extreme — the wall clamps the height: y(t,0)=y(t,a)=0.y(t,0)=y(t,a)=0.y(t,0)=y(t,a)=0.

The very same endpoint term, taken seriously, is what later reveals D-branes — see D-branes (first appearance). (The action itself is set up in Energy in string oscillations.)

    Fixing the ends also forces ∂ty=0\partial_t y=0∂t​y=0 there — the endpoints can't move if they're nailed — but that's a consequence, not a substitute for stating where the ends are pinned.

    Same frequencies, different shapes

    Every normal mode solves y′′+k2y=0y''+k^2y=0y′′+k2y=0 with k=ω/v0k=\omega/v_0k=ω/v0​ (see Normal modes and frequencies); only the endpoints select kkk. General solution y(x)=Asin⁡kx+Bcos⁡kxy(x)=A\sin kx+B\cos kxy(x)=Asinkx+Bcoskx.

    Dirichlet (y=0y=0y=0 at both ends) kills the cosine and needs sin⁡(ka)=0\sin(ka)=0sin(ka)=0: yn(x)=Ansin⁡ ⁣(nπxa),n=1,2,…y_n(x)=A_n\sin\!\Big(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\Big),\quad n=1,2,\dotsyn​(x)=An​sin(anπx​),n=1,2,…

    Both land on the identical condition ka=nπka=n\pika=nπ, so both give the same frequency tower: \boxed{\;\omega_n=\frac{n\pi v_0}{a}=\sqrt{\frac{T_0}{\mu_0}}\,\frac{n\pi}{a}.\;} \tag{1} Sine versus cosine is the only difference in shape; the nonzero frequencies coincide exactly. Dirichlet asks the height to vanish at both ends, Neumann asks the slope to — geometrically different demands that quantize kkk the same way.

    The real difference lives at n=0n=0n=0

    The two spectra part company only at the very bottom.

    • Dirichlet n=0n=0n=0: sin⁡0≡0\sin 0\equiv 0sin0≡0 — no string, no mode. Excluded.
    • Neumann n=0n=0n=0: cos⁡0≡1\cos 0\equiv 1cos0≡1 — a constant profile y0(x)=A0y_0(x)=A_0y0​(x)=A0​, the whole string shifted rigidly and ω0=0\omega_0=0ω0​=0. It isn't an oscillation; it's a rigid translation. More strongly, y(t,x)=b+uty(t,x)=b+uty(t,x)=b+ut (uniform drift, constants b,ub,ub,u) solves both the wave equation and the Neumann conditions: nothing pins a free string, so it can slide bodily in yyy.

    Physically, the Neumann zero mode is the string's center-of-mass motion in the transverse direction — the free string can be anywhere and can drift. This is the classical seed of a deep fact: for the relativistic open string, Neumann ends are the natural ones, and this zero mode becomes the string's overall position and momentum. A Dirichlet string has no such freedom; its ends are nailed, so it can neither sit wherever it likes nor drift.

    Momentum: conserved or leaking?

    Total transverse momentum is py=∫0aμ0 ∂ty dxp_y=\int_0^a \mu_0\,\partial_t y\,dxpy​=∫0a​μ0​∂t​ydx. Momentum can only change through the slope at the ends — i.e., through the transverse force the supports apply. So the two boundary conditions give opposite verdicts:

    • Neumann: ∂xy=0\partial_x y=0∂x​y=0 at both ends ⇒dpy/dt=0\Rightarrow dp_y/dt=0⇒dpy​/dt=0. Momentum is conserved — free ends push on nothing.
    • Dirichlet: the end slope is generally nonzero ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ momentum is not conserved. The walls continually shove the string; in the fundamental mode the net momentum sloshes back and forth between +y+y+y and −y-y−y.

    To see the Dirichlet leak explicitly, expand y=∑nqn(t)sin⁡(nπx/a)y=\sum_n q_n(t)\sin(n\pi x/a)y=∑n​qn​(t)sin(nπx/a). Only odd modes contribute ((−1)n−1=−2(-1)^n-1=-2(−1)n−1=−2 for odd nnn, 000 for even) — even modes are antisymmetric about the midpoint and carry zero net momentum. The moral isn't that every Dirichlet motion loses momentum; it's that the boundary condition does not enforce conservation, whereas Neumann does.

    The D-brane teaser. String theorists long distrusted Dirichlet conditions precisely because momentum leaks — where does it go, and what is this "wall"? The resolution: a Dirichlet open-string endpoint ends on a D-brane, a real dynamical object that soaks up the momentum. The wall is physical. This module plants that seed; D-branes (first appearance) grows it.

    Open questions

    • Mixed conditions (Dirichlet at one end, Neumann at the other) force ka=(n+12)πka=(n+\tfrac12)\pika=(n+21​)π and a half-integer-shifted spectrum — the exercise below works this out. They return later for strings stretched between different branes.
    • Robin conditions (c1y+c2 ∂xy=0c_1 y+c_2\,\partial_x y=0c1​y+c2​∂x​y=0, e.g. from a spring at the endpoint) interpolate between the two extremes and lie outside the fixed-wall / massless-ring models.

    Exercise

    A string on 0≤x≤a0\le x\le a0≤x≤a has a mixed setup: Dirichlet at x=0x=0x=0 (nailed) and Neumann at x=ax=ax=a (free ring).

    (a) Find the allowed mode profiles and the quantized wavenumbers kkk. (b) Write the frequency tower ωn\omega_nωn​ and say how it compares to the pure Dirichlet/Neumann tower (1). (c) Is there a zero mode? Is transverse momentum conserved?

    Solution

    Start from y(x)=Asin⁡kx+Bcos⁡kxy(x)=A\sin kx + B\cos kxy(x)=Asinkx+Bcoskx.

    (a) Dirichlet at x=0x=0x=0: y(0)=B=0y(0)=B=0y(0)=B=0, so y=Asin⁡kxy=A\sin kxy=Asinkx and y′=Akcos⁡kxy'=Ak\cos kxy′=Akcoskx. Neumann at x=ax=ax=a: y′(a)=0⇒cos⁡(ka)=0y'(a)=0\Rightarrow \cos(ka)=0y′(a)=0⇒cos(ka)=0, i.e. ka=(n+12)π,n=0,1,2,…ka=\Big(n+\tfrac12\Big)\pi,\qquad n=0,1,2,\dotska=(n+21​)π,n=0,1,2,… Profiles: yn(x)=Ansin⁡ ⁣((n+12)πxa)y_n(x)=A_n\sin\!\big(\frac{(n+\frac12)\pi x}{a}\big)yn​(x)=An​sin(a(n+21​)πx​) — these are the shapes that vanish at the nailed end and are flat at the free end (a quarter-wave for n=0n=0n=0, three-quarters for n=1n=1n=1, …).

    (b) With ω=v0k\omega=v_0 kω=v0​k, ωn=(n+12)πv0a,n=0,1,2,…\omega_n=\frac{(n+\tfrac12)\pi v_0}{a},\qquad n=0,1,2,\dotsωn​=a(n+21​)πv0​​,n=0,1,2,… The tower is shifted by a half-integer relative to (1): frequencies sit halfway between the pure-case values, and the fundamental ω0=πv0/(2a)\omega_0=\pi v_0/(2a)ω0​=πv0​/(2a) is half the pure Dirichlet fundamental πv0/a\pi v_0/aπv0​/a (a mixed string is effectively "twice as long" acoustically).

    (c) No zero mode: ka=(n+12)πka=(n+\tfrac12)\pika=(n+21​)π never allows k=0k=0k=0, and the Dirichlet end forbids the constant profile. Momentum is not conserved — the nailed x=0x=0x=0 end still has nonzero slope in general, so the wall keeps pushing. (The free end contributes nothing, but one leaking end is enough.)
