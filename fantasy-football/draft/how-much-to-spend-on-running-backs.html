How much to spend on running backs

Four named strategies that are really one question — how many of your first four picks go to running back — and what the 2026 board says the answer is.

The one axis

Every strategy below is one position on a single dial: how much of your early draft capital goes to running back. The hobby has given four points on that dial four brand names, which makes them sound like four theories. They are four answers to one question.

Why running back? It is the only position where the supply of the good version runs out inside the draft. A three-down bell cow — a back on the field on every down, collecting carries and catches, so two income streams land on one roster spot — is the scarcest asset available, and once they are gone the position does not restock. Receivers restock. Quarterbacks restock (Late-round quarterback). Backs do not.

Two terms, both in Fantasy football glossary: a carry is a run handed to a player; ADP is average draft position, where the market takes a player across thousands of mock drafts. All ADP here is Fantasy Football Calculator PPR 12-team as of 2026-07-25 — a labelled proxy for ESPN's own ADP, not ESPN's.

The four strategies, from first principles

Robust RB. Take two or three backs inside your first four picks. The logic: bell cows are the scarce thing, so buy it while it exists and accept an ordinary receiver room. You end up with a positional advantage that cannot be replicated later and a deficit that can — receivers are still being drafted in round eight.

Hero RB. Take one elite back early, then take receivers with the next several picks. One anchor, then depth everywhere else.

Anchor RB. Take one elite back early, then take receivers with the next several picks.

Those last two paragraphs are identical because Hero RB and Anchor RB are the same strategy under two names. Worth knowing on its own: the vocabulary in this hobby duplicates itself constantly, and a lot of apparent disagreement between two writers is two labels for one idea.

Zero RB. Take no back at all early. Spend the top of your draft on receivers and a move TE — a tight end split out and used as a receiver, which is where that position's entire value comes from — then attack running back afterwards, two ways: the waiver wire all season, and The injury-inheritance asymmetry — when a bell cow gets hurt, one backup typically absorbs almost the whole vacated role rather than the work spreading around, so a player unrosterable on Saturday is a weekly starter on Monday. Zero RB is not "ignore running back." It is buy running back later, in-season, at zero draft cost — and that dependence is exactly what makes it fragile.

The RB dead zone. Not a strategy. A hazard. The band of rounds where a back still costs you a real pick, but the bell cows are gone, so what you are buying is an early-down committee back — a player who runs on first and second down and comes off the field on third, missing the passing situations where full-PPR points are made. You pay starter prices for committee production.

The methodology warning — read this before you read any win rate

Almost every published strategy win rate comes from best ball

**Best ball** is a format with no waiver wire and no weekly start/sit decision: you draft, and your best scorers are counted automatically forever. It produces enormous clean datasets, which is why nearly all published "Zero RB wins X% of leagues" numbers come from it. That format **systematically misprices any strategy whose payoff depends on working the wire** — because in best ball there is no wire to work. Zero RB is exactly such a strategy: half its case is in-season acquisition. A best-ball result measures its draft-day half and silently scores the other half as zero, making it look worse than it is in a managed league and making draft-heavy strategies look better. **Cite such numbers with their format attached, or not at all.** "Zero RB posts a strong best-ball win rate" is defensible. "Zero RB wins more" is not.

A result from a different rule set is a different measurement, not a stronger version of the same one — see What basketball teaches you wrong about football.

What actually changed for 2026

Three shifts, as of 2026-07-25, all read off 2026 draft board.

Running backs are back on top. The two earliest picks in the market are both backs (ADP 1.7 and 1.8), and 13 of the first 24 picks are running backs. Through pick 12 it is an even 6–6 split with receivers; through pick 24 the backs pull ahead.

Zero RB is no longer the modal strategy. It cannot be, given that shape: taking no back in the first four rounds is a minority position when the market spends a majority of round two on backs. Zero RB in 2026 is a contrarian play — not a wrong one, but you are fading the room rather than joining it.

The dead zone has moved earlier. The last three-down bell cow the market prices at all sits at ADP 70.5 — inside round six. Data/players.csv labels 22 backs as three-down bell cows with an ADP, and every one is gone by then — though take the 22 as an upper bound rather than a count of secure jobs, since a July projection assumes a settled depth chart and the August 30 cutdown will demote several of them to committee work. What is left, in the ADP band from 50 to 105 (rounds five through nine):

  • 15 running backs. Twelve of the fifteen are early-down committee backs. Twelve of the fifteen carry a high risk flag. None is flagged low.
  • 26 wide receivers. Seventeen are flagged low risk. Exactly one is high.

That is the dead zone, measured rather than asserted — and note what it is not. It is not that those backs project badly. The 25th-ranked back projects about 200 points; a freely available back — the 29th, the Replacement level baseline this league's roster implies — projects 185. Roughly 0.9 points a week of edge for a fifth-round pick, and the receiver beside him offers a similarly thin edge. The difference is not the average, it is the floor. A committee back who loses his committee scores nothing; a receiver keeps getting thrown to.

Now make it about this league

Three settings decide this, and they mostly decide it for you.

The 1.01 is locked. The three highest-value players on the board are all three-down bell cows, and the best is worth about 10.7 points a week over a replacement back — the largest positional edge anywhere in this draft. Zero RB requires passing on that, at the one pick where the choice is least ambiguous. It is structurally unavailable from the 1.01 unless you throw away the best thing the slot gives you. See Drafting from the 1.01 and Who to take at 1.01.

The waiver reset cuts against Zero RB specifically. Waiver priority here resets every week to the inverse of the standings, so a winning team is at the back of the queue every week. Zero RB's second income stream is in-season acquisition — meaning the better your team plays, the worse your access to exactly the breakout back the strategy counts on. Losing teams get first crack. This inverts the basketball instinct that priority is a stored asset you spend when you choose: here an unspent claim expires worthless and success is taxed. Waivers has the mechanic.

The payout is convex and two-stage. First pays 500 dollars, second 250, third 150, and fourth pays exactly what twelfth pays — nothing. That rewards variance. But you must first finish top six across weeks 1–14, which rewards a high average. Resolution: do not sacrifice your average in rounds one through four; hunt upside from round seven onward.

The recommendation

Hero RB — one elite back at 1.01, then receivers and a move tight end through your next several turns — and do not touch the dead zone.

Against Andrew's pick sequence:

  • Pick 1: the bell cow. Not a strategy choice — the slot.
  • Picks 24 and 25: receivers and/or the top move TE, who sit here at a steep discount to their board value.
  • Picks 48 and 49: the last defensible running-back window — the final bell cows with real projections are priced right around here. If one is there, take him; that is your RB2. If not, take receivers and mean it.
  • Picks 72, 73, 96, 97: do not buy a running back here. This is the dead zone measured above.
  • Picks 120 onward: now buy backs in bulk, on purpose. This is where handcuffs live: backups whose value is entirely conditional on the starter getting hurt. Correctly priced only this late — and priced for one specific use. One handcuff behind your own bell cow is a cheap hedge on your most expensive asset. Hoarding handcuffs league-wide is a floor play, and this payout does not reward floor.

Robust RB is defensible from the 1.01 and is the second choice. Zero RB is the one to actively reject — not because the idea is bad, but because this league's waiver reset attacks its funding mechanism and the 1.01 makes its opening move expensive.

Open questions

  • The dead-zone boundaries are read off a July ADP proxy for a late-August draft, and the risk flags in the data are editorial judgements, not measurements. Both move at the August 30 roster cutdown; re-render before drafting.
  • The claim that Zero RB has lost modal status is inferred from ADP shape, not from a survey of what drafters say they are doing. I have no direct measurement of stated strategy mix for 2026.
  • Nobody has published a Zero RB win rate from a managed redraft league with an inverse-standings weekly waiver reset. The honest position is that this league's exact setting is untested, not that it is proven hostile.
