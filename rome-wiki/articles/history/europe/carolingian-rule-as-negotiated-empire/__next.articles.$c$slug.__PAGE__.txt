<h2 id="the-mechanism-of-rule">The mechanism of rule</h2>
<p>From about 700 the Carolingian family accumulated power within the Frankish world, then expanded by military conquest and political alliance. Counts, bishops, abbots, royal agents, assemblies, capitularies, oaths, gifts, and ecclesiastical reform connected rulers to localities. These were instruments of government, but their effectiveness depended on elite cooperation and local institutions.</p>
<p>The empire's Roman and Christian language should not conceal its negotiated operation. Coinage, titles, cities, roads, and church organization reused late-Roman inheritances; conquest also generated resistance and regional accommodation. Fragmentation in the ninth century did not simply switch “central authority” off: it rearranged political networks whose social changes continued beyond the dynasty.</p>
<h2 id="place-in-the-map">Place in the map</h2>
<p>This is the western comparison to <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/europe/byzantium-as-a-continuing-roman-polity/" class="wiki-link">Byzantium as a continuing Roman polity</a> beneath <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/europe/transformation-of-the-roman-world/" class="wiki-link">Transformation of the Roman world</a>. It also provides an institutional prerequisite for <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/europe/feudalism-debate/" class="wiki-link">Feudalism debate</a>: later lordship cannot be explained only as the residue of Carolingian collapse.</p>
