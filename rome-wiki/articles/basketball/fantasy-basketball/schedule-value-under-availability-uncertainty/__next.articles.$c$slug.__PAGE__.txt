<h2 id="why-a-schedule-count-is-not-a-forecast">Why a schedule count is not a forecast</h2>
<p>An NBA schedule is a set of dated opportunities. It establishes that a team is supposed to play, but not that a player will be active, retain his projected workload, fit a fantasy lineup, or deliver the same per-minute role. Calling four games “better” than three silently assigns probability one to several uncertain conversions. That shortcut is sometimes harmless for a healthy star in a spacious daily lineup. It becomes dangerous near injuries, back-to-backs, weekly locks, late-season rotation changes, or crowded roster dates.</p>

<h2 id="separate-four-gates">Separate four gates</h2>
<p>The first gate is whether the game occurs. Postponements and schedule amendments are uncommon but real; use the current official schedule and record the retrieval time. A third-party grid is convenient discovery, not canonical truth.</p>
<p>The second gate is whether the player participates. Official injury reports are timestamped status disclosures. “Questionable” is not a universal probability, “available” is not a guarantee of normal minutes, and absence from an early report may not settle a later update. A forecast should state which report it uses and when the next report can overturn it.</p>
<p>The third gate is workload and role. A player can dress and still have reduced minutes, fewer possessions, no closing role, or a changed statistical mix. Returning from injury, joining a new rotation, or playing through a restriction can make the active-game count look right while the production forecast remains wrong. Model workload states rather than multiplying full-strength per-game value by an active probability.</p>
<p>The fourth gate is fantasy usability. A real appearance is worthless to the matchup if it is benched because eligible slots are full, arrives after a weekly lock, exceeds a games cap, or displaces a superior start. Lineup fit is roster-specific. Leaguewide schedule rankings cannot observe it.</p>
<p>These gates are conditional. If a player is ruled out, role and lineup assignment vanish. If he is limited, the manager may deliberately bench him, making <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>L</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">L</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal">L</span></span></span></span> depend on <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>W</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">W</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.1389em;">W</span></span></span></span>. Multiplying four unconditional averages can therefore create a false sense of rigor.</p>
<h2 id="joint-states-are-safer-than-independent-games">Joint states are safer than independent games</h2>
<p>Suppose a player has four scheduled games. Assigning each an independent 80 percent participation probability yields 3.2 expected active games, but it understates tail risk when the same injury drives all four outcomes. A setback can erase the week; successful return can preserve most of it. The mean may remain 3.2 while the chance of zero or one game is much larger than the binomial model says.</p>

<p>Back-to-backs deserve similar treatment. Do not label the second game an automatic rest. Build branches such as both games, first only, second only, and neither, then ask whether team and player evidence supports unequal probabilities. The pair is correlated through fatigue, medical management, and the result of the first game.</p>
<p>Shared team risks create portfolio correlation. Two players from the same club share postponement, travel, blowout, and some rotation conditions. Two players whose roles depend on the same injured teammate can move in opposite directions when that teammate returns. Adding their independent expected games misses the concentration. A roster with equal mean schedule value but less shared downside may have the better survival profile.</p>
<h2 id="expected-games-are-not-enough">Expected games are not enough</h2>
<p>Expected active games compress a distribution into one number. That number is useful for additive season-long planning, but head-to-head decisions often care about thresholds and tails. A player with outcomes of zero or four games can have the same mean as a player nearly certain to play two. If the matchup needs a minimum floor, the second is safer; if only a high-output branch can overturn a large deficit, the first may preserve more upside.</p>
<p>Report at least three features:</p>
<ol>
<li>expected playable games;</li>
<li>probability of falling below the minimum useful number; and</li>
<li>plausible production range conditional on playing.</li>
</ol>
<p>For category leagues, convert states into category-margin effects. More uncertain games do not affect all categories proportionally. A blocks specialist may have a high chance of zero blocks even when active, and percentage impact depends on makes and attempts. For points leagues, use the exact scoring formula and workload state. For roto, compare the distribution with remaining games caps and standings thresholds.</p>

<h2 id="information-timing-changes-schedule-value">Information timing changes schedule value</h2>
<p>The same player can have different value in daily and weekly-lock leagues because information arrives at different times. If the lineup locks Monday and the decisive injury update comes Tuesday, the update cannot rescue the assignment. The relevant forecast is the distribution at Monday's information set, not the distribution after the news.</p>
<p>Daily moves preserve conditional actions. A manager may hold through an afternoon report, place an eligible player in IL, or choose a replacement before tip. That flexibility has value only if platform processing and candidate availability allow the pivot. Yahoo's official help distinguishes same-day, next-day, and weekly transaction timing; a nominal replacement is not feasible if it activates after the useful game.</p>
<p>Define an information checkpoint for every uncertain game: next official injury report, shootaround reporting, lineup confirmation, waiver processing, and lineup lock. Then ask whether a decision can change at that checkpoint. Information arriving after irreversibility may improve understanding but not the current choice.</p>
<p>Waiting also has a cost. A replacement may be claimed, an early game may pass, or the uncertain player may become ineligible for an IL move. Compare act now, wait, and contingent-action paths. <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/basketball/fantasy-basketball/multi-move-streaming-paths/" class="wiki-link">Multi-move streaming paths</a> supplies the sequential framework: the best plan is a policy that says what to do after each update, not a fixed string of assumed transactions.</p>
<h2 id="replacement-paths-absorb-uncertainty">Replacement paths absorb uncertainty</h2>

An IL slot, liquid bench position, multi-position eligibility, and abundant waiver pool can turn an uncertain player into a call option: keep the upside while retaining a practical fallback. A weekly-locked injured player with no late swap has far less recoverability.</p>

<h2 id="a-reproducible-workflow">A reproducible workflow</h2>
<ol>
<li>Freeze scoring periods, lineup locks, caps, eligibility, waiver timing, acquisition limits, and IL rules from the live league.</li>
<li>Load the current official NBA schedule and injury report; record timestamps.</li>
<li>Build a day-by-day lineup grid and identify which games would actually improve the legal lineup.</li>
<li>Create three to five joint availability/workload states from mechanisms, not labels alone.</li>
<li>Assign broad probability ranges and name the evidence that could move them.</li>
<li>Translate every state through the exact points formula or category-margin objective.</li>
<li>Add feasible replacement paths and transaction costs state by state.</li>
<li>Compare expected value, downside threshold, and upside branch rather than one schedule count.</li>
<li>Test correlated absences, one missed back-to-back game, a minutes limit, and crowded-night displacement.</li>
<li>Set the expiry at the next report, lock, transaction, or game.</li>

<h2 id="failure-modes">Failure modes</h2>
<p><strong>Multiplying games by a single active probability.</strong> This ignores workload states, game dependence, and lineup fit.</p>
<p><strong>Treating official status as prognosis.</strong> The injury report establishes a disclosure, not a universal numeric probability or minutes forecast.</p>
<p><strong>Assuming independence.</strong> One injury, teammate return, or team decision can affect several games and players together.</p>
<p><strong>Counting recoverability that does not exist.</strong> A fallback after lock is not an option.</p>
<p><strong>Using expected value alone.</strong> Equal means can conceal very different elimination risk.</p>
<p><strong>Double-discounting.</strong> If a projection already embeds expected games or limited minutes, do not apply the same reduction again.</p>
<p><strong>Ignoring the alternative.</strong> Four uncertain games are not compared with zero; they are compared with the best feasible roster path.</p>
<p><strong>Converting incentives into claims.</strong> Late-season standings or a back-to-back can justify wider uncertainty, not an unsupported assertion that a player will rest.</p>
<h2 id="why-it-matters">Why it matters</h2>
<p>Availability uncertainty does not merely subtract a fraction from the schedule. It changes the shape, correlation, timing, and recoverability of fantasy output. A defensible model begins with official calendar facts, builds joint participation and workload states, passes them through legal lineup capacity, and compares complete replacement paths.</p>
<p>The practical result is often simpler than the model: prefer the player whose relevant games are likely to be active, playable, and recoverable under the league's rules. But reaching that sentence honestly requires separating what the NBA has scheduled, what evidence suggests may happen, and what the fantasy roster can still do when reality changes.</p>
<p>That separation also makes later forecast review possible without rewriting the original information set.</p>
