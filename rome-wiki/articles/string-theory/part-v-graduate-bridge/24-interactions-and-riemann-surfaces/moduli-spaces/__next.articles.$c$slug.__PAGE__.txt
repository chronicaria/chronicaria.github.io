3:T173b7,<p>You are handed a surface — a sphere, a disk — with some marked points on it, the punctures where strings enter and leave. Two such surfaces count as <em>the same</em> if a conformal map slides one onto the other. So the honest question is never "where are the punctures?" but "which puncture configurations are genuinely different?" Conformal symmetry lets you drag a few punctures to standard spots for free; whatever positions you <em>still can't remove</em> are the <strong>moduli</strong>. Counting them is pure bookkeeping: <strong>moduli = (things to place) − (symmetry to spend).</strong></p>

<h2 id="how-much-symmetry-can-you-spend">How much symmetry can you spend?</h2>
<p>The symmetry group is the set of conformal maps a surface has to <em>itself</em> (its global automorphisms). The size of this group is exactly the number of puncture-placements you get for free.</p>

<p><strong>Boundary maps preserve cyclic order.</strong> Differentiating (2) along the real axis,
<span class="katex-error" title="ParseError: KaTeX parse error: \tag works only in display equations" style="color:#cc0000">\frac{dw}{dz}=\frac{ad-bc}{(cz+d)^2}=\frac{1}{(cz+d)^2}>0. \tag{4}</span>
The boundary parametrization moves strictly forward, so a real Möbius map can <em>slide</em> three ordered boundary punctures anywhere but can <strong>never reverse their cyclic order</strong>. This constraint has no analog for interior punctures on the sphere, and it is what makes open-string moduli richer (discrete data on top of continuous).</p>

<h2 id="why-this-matters-for-amplitudes">Why this matters for amplitudes</h2>
<p>The amplitude is an integral <strong>over the moduli space</strong>, not over the surfaces themselves. Each conformally-inequivalent surface is one "history" connecting the incoming and outgoing strings; summing all histories means integrating <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>λ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\lambda</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal">λ</span></span></span></span> over its range.</p>
<p>The contrast with ordinary field theory is sharp. In particle QFT an interaction is a <strong>point</strong>: several worldlines meet at a vertex, and you assign that single point a single number (the coupling). A string worldsheet has <em>no such point</em> — locally it always looks like a free string, and the "interaction" is a global feature of the shape. So there is nothing point-like to hand a coupling to. Instead the interaction is <strong>smeared across a whole one-parameter family</strong> of conformally-inequivalent worldsheets, and the only invariant way to sum their contributions is to integrate the modulus.</p>
<p>That is why the four-string tree amplitude is <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math class="mopen">(</span><span class="minner">…</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.1667em;"></span><span class="mclose">)</span></span></span></span> and not a bare vertex factor: there is a one-dimensional quotient space of inequivalent four-punctured surfaces (<span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math
