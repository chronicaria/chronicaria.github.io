The last two rounds — kicker and defence

Two lineup slots you must fill and are not allowed to care about — plus the one thing at the end of the draft that is actually worth thinking hard about.

Permission not to care

Your lineup starts one kicker and one defence-and-special-teams unit (D-ST — you roster a whole team's defence, never an individual defender) every single week. Twelve teams, so twelve of each are started league-wide.

The standard finding across the industry — not a number computed from this build's data — is that only about ten defences and about eleven kickers project above a freely replaceable alternative. Fewer units clear the bar than there are slots to fill. At least one team every week is starting a negative asset, and there is no draft pick that fixes that. It is a property of the position, not of your preparation.

So the arithmetic on the board is what it is. Read out of the "Replacement baselines used" table on the 2026 draft board, computed from Data/players.csv on 2026-07-25: the best kicker is worth about 1.8 points a week over a typical (12th-ranked) starter and the best defence about 3.4 — both figures modelled, not observed; see the flags below. The best running back is worth 10.7 over the last back you could still start. Weekly margins in this league routinely run under ten points. Kicker and defence are rounding errors you are contractually obliged to purchase.

Buy them at the end and spend nothing on the decision. Your last three picks are 168, 169 and 192 — the back-to-back turn at the end of round 14 and the start of round 15, and then the final pick of the draft. Take the kicker and the defence at 168 and 169. Pick 192 has a better job, and the end of this note is about that job.

Player archetypes gives neither position a label on purpose. A kicker's value comes from the offense he plays for; a defence's comes from who it is playing that week. Neither is a thing you own — both are things you rent, weekly. That is also why the 2026 draft board's turn map leaves them out of its columns entirely: they are taken by rule, not because value says so. See Streaming a position.

Instruction one — the defence rule, and why every published ranking is wrong here

This is the part where you cannot use anyone else's rankings.

Almost every fantasy league scores a defence on points allowed plus event bonuses — sacks, interceptions, fumble recoveries, touchdowns. This league adds active yards-allowed brackets on top, and they are steep. Confirmed from Andrew's own ESPN settings screen on 2026-07-25:

Total yards allowed Points
Under 100 +5
100–199 +3
200–299 +2
350–399 −1
400–449 −3
450–499 −5
500–549 −6
550 or more −7

Two further departures from the stock ESPN scale: the points-allowed brackets are flatter — a shutout pays 5 here rather than the old scale's 10 — and a safety pays 4 rather than 2.

Flatter on the upside, far heavier on the downside. Here is the sentence that matters: a defence that allows 46 or more points takes −5, and if it also allows 550 or more yards it takes another −7. That is −12 before it has recorded a single sack. Under points-allowed-only scoring the same catastrophe costs about −5. The negative tail in this league is roughly double what any published ranking assumes.

The instruction that follows is a streaming rule, and it is sharper than the one you will read anywhere else:

Avoid elite offences far more aggressively than standard advice suggests.

Standard advice treats a bad matchup as a low-scoring week. Here it is a genuinely destructive week — a defence can hand back more than a mediocre wide receiver gains you. When you set the lineup each week, the question is not "is this a good defence" but "is the offense it faces capable of putting up 450 yards." If yes, start something else. See Implied team total.

One honest flag. The Proj pts figures in the D/ST rows of the 2026 draft board are modelled, not observed — they are computed from projected yards and points allowed run through the scale above, precisely because no published source prices these brackets. The board says so in its own warning callout. Read those rows as an ordering of defences, not as a forecast of their scores.

Instruction two — the kicker rule

First, what a kicker is being paid for. A field goal is the consolation prize for a drive that stalls short of the end zone: the offense kicks the ball through the uprights from wherever it got stuck, and the further out it starts the harder the kick. An extra point (PAT) is the near-automatic short kick taken immediately after a touchdown. So a kicker is paid for both outcomes of the offense in front of him: drives that stall produce field goals, drives that finish produce extra points.

Kicking is close to unpredictable by kicker and somewhat predictable by team context. Nobody has ever reliably forecast which kicker makes his kicks. What is forecastable is how often a kicker gets sent out at all, and that is a property of his offense: an offense that moves the ball but stalls before the end zone produces field goals, and an offense that scores a lot produces extra points and long-range attempts.

So the rule is: stream by the offense's Implied team total — the points a betting market expects that team to score — not by the kicker's name. The name on the board is a label for an offense.

This league adds one wrinkle worth knowing. Confirmed 2026-07-25: field goals pay 3 from inside 40 yards, 4 from 40–49, 5 from 50–59 and 6 from 60 or more, the extra point after a touchdown pays 1, and any missed field goal is −1. That last rule is unusual — most formats charge nothing for a miss.

The consequence: a high-volume inaccurate kicker is worse than his raw make total suggests. A kicker who attempts many kicks and misses several is paying a tax his make count does not show. So the rule is not "ignore attempt volume" — volume is the one forecastable thing about the position. It is chase attempt volume, then discount it for a kicker who misses often, because every miss costs a point here. When two kickers look alike on volume, take the accurate one.

One flag, the same one the defence rows carry. The Proj pts figures in the K rows of the 2026 draft board — and the 1.8 points a week above — are modelled, not observed. The source projection gives makes and attempts with no distances, so this league's 3/4/5/6-by-distance scale is applied through an assumed league-average distance mix, meaning every made field goal is valued identically. Read those rows as an ordering of kickers, not as a forecast of their scores.

Also worth reversing while you are here: the old wisdom that you should secure a good defence early is dead. The top-drafted defence has not finished the season as the number-one defence in over a decade. The unit everyone agrees on in August is essentially never the right unit in December, which is the whole argument for renting rather than buying.

Instruction three — what pick 192 is actually for

Your seven bench spots have to cover bye weeks for nine starters, plus handcuffs, plus whatever speculative adds you want to hold. That is tight — see Bye-week planning.

But the IR slot is enabled, and it does not consume a bench spot. It is a sixteenth-plus-one roster space that exists only if you put something in it.

The final pick of the draft — pick 192 — should be the highest-upside player who will open the season on IR.

A genuinely injured, high-ceiling asset. He goes straight into the IR slot rather than onto your bench, so **he costs you nothing at all**, and this league's top-three-only payout rewards exactly that kind of lottery ticket. Only players carrying an **O** or **IR** designation may occupy the slot; **suspended players may not.** Verify the designation on ESPN before you take him — a player who is merely questionable will land on your bench and cost you a real spot.

This is the one instinct that transfers cleanly from fantasy basketball: an IL slot is free option value, and it works the same way here.

Corollary, and it is not optional: never roster a second kicker or a second D/ST. The positional maximums permit three kickers and five defences. Seven bench spots do not. A second kicker is a bench spot spent on a position worth a modelled 1.8 points a week — the single most expensive way to feel prepared. If your defence has a bad matchup, you drop it and claim another. That is what the waiver wire is for.

Open questions

  • The "roughly ten defences and eleven kickers" figure is received industry wisdom, not something computed from Data/players.csv. The direction is certainly right; the exact count is not verified for this scoring system.
  • The magnitude of the yards-allowed correction is unverified. No published ranking prices these brackets, so there is nothing external to check the modelled D/ST rows against — only the direction is safe.
  • Whether a specific injured player is IR-eligible at pick 192 depends on his designation on draft day, which will not be settled until the August 30 roster cutdown. This note gives the rule, not the name.
