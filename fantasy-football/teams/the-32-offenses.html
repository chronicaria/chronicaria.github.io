Why this is one table and not thirty-two notes

The full version of this exercise is to model each team's touch budget — the fixed number of carries and targets an offense will produce, split into shares across its players (The conserved team touch budget, Opportunity share). It is a real technique, and the payoff is real: you find players the market has priced wrong.

But that payoff is exactly the thing a first-time drafter should buy from consensus rather than build. You have no way to check a share estimate against what a coach will actually do, and a wrong share estimate feels identical to a right one. So this desk carries the cheap, load-bearing parts — the bye, the handful of players who matter, and a one-line scheme signal — and skips the thirteen hundred allocation rows.

Pace and pass-lean matter here in a way they do not in basketball

Your basketball instinct says pace is a multiplier: a fast team runs maybe 8% more possessions than a slow one, everyone's box score inflates a little, and it barely moves a ranking.

Football is not like that. An NFL team runs somewhere around 60 offensive plays a game, and a fast, pass-leaning offense can run meaningfully more of them than a slow, run-leaning one — and each pass play creates a target (a throw in a receiver's direction, see Fantasy football glossary) while each run creates a carry. Pace and pass-lean therefore determine how many scoring opportunities exist at all before anyone's share of them is argued about. In basketball everyone on the floor shares the same inflated pie. In football the size of the pie and its split between running and throwing are set by the play-caller, and they decide which positions on that team are even worth rostering.

That is why a new coordinator moves an offense's fantasy output more than a new player does, and why the last column below is a scheme line rather than a talent line.

Two of these columns are data. Two are estimates.

**Bye** is read out of `Data/players.csv` and verified programmatically — trust it. **The 2–4 players who matter** are that team's highest `proj_points` players on the draft board, with the `archetype` label straight from the same file — trust it. Kickers and defences are excluded on purpose; you stream those by Implied team total, not by team. **Pace** is a judgement call at low confidence. Where I could not form one honestly, it says `unclear`. **Pass-lean** is a coarse tercile of a proxy — the share of each team's projected points coming from receivers and tight ends rather than running backs. That is a projection artefact, not observed play-calling. **OL tier** (offensive line — the five blockers, whose quality persists across seasons more than almost anything else in football) is judgement at low confidence, `unclear` where I have nothing. Do not treat the middle two columns as measurements. They are context for reading the fourth column, not inputs to a decision.

Byes were checked against Data/players.csv on 2026-07-25. Every team's bye below matches the bye value on all of that team's rows. Byes fall only in weeks 5–11, 13 and 14, and week 11 is the crunch with six teams off (Atlanta, Cleveland, Green Bay, Los Angeles Rams, New England, Seattle) — see Bye-week planning.

How to actually use this

Three uses, in order of value.

  1. Bye planning. Before the draft, and again after it, count how many of your starters share a bye. Week 11 is the one to watch. Nothing else in this table is as reliable.
  2. Sanity-checking a pick. If the board offers you a running back and this table says his team is a committee behind a bottom-third line, that is a reason to prefer the next name. It is not a reason to override the board by more than a pick or two.
  3. Reading a depth chart. When you look a team up in Reading an NFL depth chart, this row tells you whether the offense throws enough for its third receiver to matter.

What this table is not for is finding value the market missed. That is what the touch-budget model does, and you should not be running it this year.

Open questions

  • The confidence gap between columns is real and uncomfortable. Bye and the player list are read out of players.csv and verified; pace and OL tier are my judgement, and the pass-lean tercile is a proxy computed from projections rather than from play-calling. If a pace or line estimate contradicts a consensus source, believe the source, not this table.
  • Coordinator and play-caller assignments for 2026 could not be confirmed against a primary source, so the last column describes the shape the projections imply rather than naming a scheme with a person attached. That is the weakest claim in the note.
  • Depth charts move at the August 30 cutdown, and the "players who matter" list is a snapshot of proj_points on 2026-07-25. A committee resolving into a bell cow between now and the draft would change several rows.
