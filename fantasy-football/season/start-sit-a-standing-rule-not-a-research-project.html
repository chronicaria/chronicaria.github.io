Start-sit — a standing rule, not a research project

Setting your lineup is a rule you apply seventeen times, not a question you re-open every week.

as of 2026-07-25 · status seed · format espn-ppr1.0-12tm-std

Up: Fantasy Football · Related: Implied team total · Reading the NFL injury report · The fantasy football week · Sample size in a 17-game season · How the league is won

The rule

Your first five picks start every week regardless of matchup. The only live decisions are FLEX, kicker and defence. Decide all three by Implied team total. Never start a Questionable player whose backup kicks off in an earlier game window.

Write it down once. Apply it in about four minutes each Sunday morning. Everything below is why each clause is there.

"Your first five picks start every week regardless of matchup"

You spent real draft capital on those five because they are the players who get the ball most — a three-down bell cow who both runs and catches, an alpha X who draws targets whatever the coverage, a move TE at a position where almost nobody else is even used as a receiver. Target (a throw in a player's direction, counted whether or not he catches it) and carry volume are the stickiest things in football. See Fantasy football glossary for both terms.

The temptation is to bench one of them in a bad matchup. Do not. A "bad matchup" moves a good player's expected score by a point or two; sitting him for a bench player moves it by six or eight in the wrong direction. The matchup effect is real but it is small compared to the role gap, and the role gap is exactly what you paid for. Your best players are also your least uncertain players. Uncertainty is the thing you are trying to remove from the lineup card, not add to it.

"The only live decisions are FLEX, kicker and defence"

FLEX is a starting slot that will accept a running back, receiver or tight end. It is the one place where you genuinely have two similar-quality options, because the alternative is a bench player and the bench is where the coin-flips live.

Kicker and defence are decisions every week because you do not really own a kicker or a defence — you own a slot that you refill. Both are best treated as streamed positions: you play whoever has the best situation this week and you drop them when the situation changes. The per-week edge of the best kicker over a typical twelfth-ranked starter is 1.8 points, and for defence 3.4 points, computed from Data/players.csv on 2026-07-25 (kicker and D/ST projections there are modelled, not observed). Small numbers — which is the point. You are not chasing greatness at those slots, you are avoiding the bottom of the range, and one number does that for you.

"Decide all three by implied team total"

Implied team total is the number of points Vegas expects a specific team to score, pulled out of the betting line. It updates itself, costs nothing, and requires no football knowledge at all to read. For all three live decisions it points the same way:

  • FLEX — take the player on the offense expected to score more.
  • Kicker — take the kicker on a high-implied-total offense, because kickers are paid by their offense's field position and scoring chances, not by their own skill.
  • Defence — take the defence facing the lowest implied total, because a defence scores by stopping an offense that was not going to do much anyway.

One number, three answers. For someone who is never going to watch a game, this substitutes for the film study you are not going to do. Full mechanics in Implied team total.

Game script, in one line

A leading team runs the ball, and a trailing team throws it. That is the whole concept. Game script is the way the expected shape of a game redistributes touches: if a team is expected to be ahead, its running backs gain work and its receivers lose some; if it is expected to be behind, its passing game gains and its backs lose carries but pick up catches. In full PPR — every reception worth a full point, see Fantasy football glossary — trailing is much less damaging to a pass-catching back than it sounds. Implied team total plus the spread tells you which side of this a player is on, and it is the only reason you would ever break the "start your best five" rule — and even then, do not.

"Never start a Questionable player whose backup kicks off earlier"

The NFL publishes an official weekly injury report with defined terms — Questionable is roughly a coin flip, Doubtful is 25% or less, Out means he will not play (Reading the NFL injury report). Final Sunday inactives land about 90 minutes before kickoff.

Here is the trap. Lineups in this league lock individually, at each player's own kickoff. So if your Questionable starter plays at 4:25pm and his replacement option plays at 1:00pm, you must choose before you know anything, and a scratch gives you a zero in that slot. If the ordering is reversed — the replacement plays later — the risk is free, because you can wait. So the rule is not "never start a Questionable player." It is: only take that risk when the fallback kicks off after him. Check the kickoff times before the injury status, not the other way round.

Why it is worth obeying

This is where fantasy basketball misleads you badly, so name it plainly. In an 82-game NBA season, a bad start-sit call costs you a fraction of one game out of 82, and the schedule self-corrects — you get the player back in two days and the error washes out over a hundred lineup decisions.

Football has fourteen regular-season games before the playoffs. A bad start-sit call is not a small drag on a large sample; it is one loss out of fourteen, and it does not wash out, because there is no next game this week to make it back. Six of twelve teams make the playoffs. Teams miss by one game routinely. See Sample size in a 17-game season and How the league is won.

That cuts both ways, and it is the actual argument for the rule rather than for weekly research. If a single decision can cost you the season, the correct response is not to think harder about each one — thinking harder mostly produces the confident, clever, wrong benching of your second-round pick. It is to make the decision the same way every week, so the number of ways you can lose stays small.

Open questions

  • The claim that matchup effects are smaller than role gaps is a general prior, not something measured against 2026 data. A genuinely extreme defensive matchup at a thin position could be worth more than a point or two.
  • FLEX decisions between two players with near-identical implied totals have no tiebreaker in this rule. Snap share or recent target volume would be the natural next input, and this note deliberately does not add one.
  • Kicker and D/ST edge figures come from modelled projections in players.csv, so the "small edge" conclusion is only as good as that model.
