a:T85fa,<h2 id="1-heat-index--summer-nws-rothfusz-regression">1. Heat index — summer (NWS Rothfusz regression)</h2>
<p>The US <strong>heat index</strong> combines <strong>air temperature and relative humidity</strong> only (no wind). It's a multiple-regression fit by Lans P. Rothfusz in a 1990 NWS technical attachment (SR 90-23) to Steadman's 1979 apparent-temperature table.</p>

<p><strong>Valid range:</strong> the regression is only meaningful for <strong>T ≳ 80 °F and RH ≳ 40%</strong>; below a computed HI of ~80 °F, NWS falls back to a simpler Steadman-based formula. It is explicitly <em>not</em> valid beyond the range of Steadman's data.</p>
<p><strong>Worked points (NWS heat-index chart, shade values):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>90 °F + 70% RH → <strong>≈ 106 °F</strong> feels-like (the canonical example).</li>
<li>80 °F + 40% RH → ~80 °F (barely any bump — low humidity).</li>
<li>100 °F + 50% RH → ~120 °F.</li>
<li>Full sun can add <strong>up to +15 °F</strong> over these shade values.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="2-wind-chill--winter-nws--environment-canada-2001">2. Wind chill — winter (NWS / Environment Canada 2001)</h2>
<p>The <strong>wind chill temperature (WCT)</strong> combines <strong>air temperature and wind speed</strong> only (no humidity), modeling convective heat loss from bare skin. Jointly adopted by NWS and Environment Canada in 2001, replacing the 1945 Siple–Passel index, and calibrated to human facial-cooling trials (T in °F, V in mph):</p>

<p><strong>Worked points (NWS):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>20 °F + 20 mph → <strong>≈ 1 °F</strong> (a common table value of ~4 °F traces to the older 1945 Siple–Passel index; the 2001 formula gives ~1 °F).</li>
<li>0 °F + 15 mph → <strong>−19 °F</strong> (NWS: exposed skin can freeze in ~30 min).</li>
</ul>
<aside class="callout callout-note">
<p class="callout-title">Note</p>
<p>Two different formulas
Heat index and wind chill are <strong>disjoint</strong> — different variables (humidity vs wind), different seasons, no overlap in valid range (80 °F+ vs 50 °F−). This is exactly why a single year-round index is convenient. See §3.</p>
</aside>
<h2 id="3-steadman-apparent-temperature--the-year-round-feels-like">3. Steadman apparent temperature — the year-round "feels like"</h2>
<p>Robert Steadman's <strong>apparent temperature (AT)</strong>, formalized in his 1984 <em>A Universal Scale of Apparent Temperature</em>, is what <strong>Australia's BoM</strong> and <strong>Open-Meteo</strong> report as "feels like." Unlike the US pair, one formula covers <strong>hot and cold</strong> and includes <strong>temperature, humidity AND wind together</strong> (optionally solar radiation).</p>

<p><strong>How it differs from heat index:</strong> heat index ignores wind and only works when it's hot; AT subtracts a <strong>wind-cooling term</strong> so it also captures wind chill, making it valid <strong>year-round</strong>. BoM's own examples: −1 °C can <em>feel like</em> −15 °C in alpine wind; 29 °C can <em>feel like</em> 38 °C in the humid tropics. BoM's standard AT does <strong>not</strong> include direct sun (which can add ~+8 °C midday) and assumes appropriate clothing.</p>
<h2 id="4-why-feels-like-differs-across-a-city-even-at-the-same-air-temp">4. Why feels-like differs across a city even at the same air temp</h2>
<p><a href="/rome-wiki/articles/weather/temperature-forecast-confidence-intervals/" class="wiki-link">Temperature</a> is the <em>most</em> spatially variable input — urban studies measure up to ~13 °C differences across a city <em>at the same minute</em> (asphalt vs shade vs water). <strong>Relative humidity is more spatially uniform</strong> — it tracks broadly with proximity to water and vegetation rather than fine-grained surface cover. So:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Waterfront / park / vegetated</strong> microclimates run <strong>more humid</strong> → in summer, a <em>higher</em> heat index even where the thermometer reads the same.</li>
<li>Because AT and heat index are <strong>nonlinear</strong> in humidity, two neighborhoods at identical air temp can still post different "feels like" numbers — humidity and local wind do the splitting.</li>
</ul>
<p>Practical upshot for the <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/weather/nyc-weather-dashboard/" class="wiki-link">NYC weather dashboard</a>: a single grid-point <code>apparent_temperature</code> is a neighborhood approximation; waterfront blocks (Battery, the piers) skew more humid/muggier than an inland grid cell suggests.</p>
<h2 id="5-open-meteo-specifics-for-the-dashboard">5. Open-Meteo specifics (for the dashboard)</h2>
<p>Open-Meteo's <code>apparent_temperature</code> <strong>is the Steadman AT</strong> (radiation form), computed as:</p>
<pre><code>e   = rh/100 * 6.105 * exp(17.27*T / (237.7 + T))   // Tetens vapour pressure
ws2 = wind_speed_10m * 0.75                          // 10 m → ~2 m
Q   = max(0, 0.1 * (shortwave_radiation - 550))      // capped body-absorbed radiation
AT  = T + 0.348*e - 0.70*ws2 + 0.70*(Q/(ws2+10)) - 4.25
</code></pre>
<ul>
<li>It reports <strong>one unified <code>apparent_temperature</code></strong>, <em>not</em> separate heat-index / wind-chill fields — the Steadman form already spans both seasons.</li>
<li>The 550 W/m² radiation floor is a deliberate cap (raw noon sun ~1100 W/m² otherwise produces absurd values).</li>
<li><strong>Per-member availability:</strong> yes — <code>apparent_temperature</code> is a listed <strong>Ensemble API</strong> hourly variable, returned for <strong>every ensemble member</strong> (<code>apparent_temperature_memberNN</code>). So the <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/weather/nyc-weather-dashboard/" class="wiki-link">NYC weather dashboard</a> can draw a widening feels-like band exactly like the temperature band, not just a single line.</li>
</ul>
