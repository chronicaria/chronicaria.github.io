The 2026 season calendar

Seventeen games squeezed into eighteen weeks, which is why every team disappears for one of them — and why the calendar is a draft-day constraint, not a thing you look up in October.

The shape of the year

The 2026 NFL regular season opens Wednesday, September 9, 2026 and runs 18 weeks. Each team plays 17 games. The missing week is the bye — one scheduled week off, per team, per season. See Fantasy football glossary.

Our league sits inside that:

  • Weeks 1–14: fantasy regular season. Head-to-head, 14 matchups, with the top 6 of 10 qualifying.
  • Weeks 15–17: the playoffs. A six-team bracket. Three weeks, win them all.
  • Week 18 does not exist for us. The NFL plays it; fantasy has already paid out. Some teams with settled playoff positions may also rest starters, making Week 18 especially volatile even if it counted.

Counting forward from the opener, weeks 15, 16 and 17 land roughly December 17 through January 4. Hold onto that; it comes back at the end of this note.

The two-stage structure is the strategic story: weeks 1–14 reward a high average score, weeks 15–17 reward a high ceiling, and those two things want different rosters.

Byes: the 2026 distribution

Byes fall only in weeks 5–11, 13 and 14. There are none in weeks 1–4, none in week 12, and — importantly — none in weeks 15–17. The fantasy playoffs are bye-free.

Week 11 is the crunch. Six teams off — nearly one player in five across the league is unavailable that week, and no other week comes close. It is the one date on this calendar worth memorising before the draft.

After that the picture is flatter than it is usually described. Weeks 6, 7, 8, 10 and 13 each take exactly four teams off, so week 6 is one of five weeks tied at four teams off, not the second-worst week. What makes week 6 the one to watch among those five is timing: it arrives before you have any waiver history, before injuries have reshuffled anyone's roster, and before you know which of your late picks are real.

Two smaller landmines: weeks 13 and 14 have byes at all. Those are the last two weeks of the fantasy regular season, when seeding is decided and a loss can cost you the playoffs outright. Losing a starter to a scheduled absence in week 14 is a worse outcome than losing one in week 6, for the same absence.

Why this binds the draft

Nine starters. Seven bench spots. That is the entire constraint.

Across weeks 1–14 you need to fill 9 starting slots 14 times — 126 starts. Every player contributes at most 13 of his possible 14, because one week he is simply not playing. That is a known zero on a known date.

The failure mode is concentration. If three of your nine starters share a bye week, that week you are starting three bench players drafted for upside rather than reliability. A single bad week in a 14-week race is survivable; two of them, plus normal bad luck, is not.

The structure of your own draft makes this easy to get wrong. You pick at 1, 20, 21, 40, 41, 60, 61, 80, 81, 100, 101, 120, 121, 140, 141, 160 — back-to-back at every turn after the first. Taking two players in consecutive picks without glancing at their bye weeks is the single cheapest mistake available, and you get seven chances to make it. Reading the draft room covers the mechanics of managing those pairs.

The correct weight, though, is light. Bye weeks are a tiebreaker, not a criterion. Never pass on a clearly better player to fix a bye collision in rounds 1–6; do check it from round 7 onward, where players are close enough in value that it is a free consideration. Bye-week planning handles the in-season side and Bye weeks as a roster constraint does the underlying reasoning.

A basketball false friend. In fantasy basketball, schedule is a volume lever you pull weekly — games-per-week, back-to-backs, streaming a spare slot for an extra game. There are 82 games and no byes, so schedule is something you exploit. Football has 17 games, one guaranteed zero, and a single game per week per player. The schedule here is not an edge to farm; it is a hole to cover. And you cannot reliably cover it from the wire, because this league's waiver order resets every week to the inverse of the standings — if you are winning, you have the worst claim on the replacement you need. See Waivers.

Why the playoff dates matter

Weeks 15–17 fall from roughly mid-December to the first weekend of January, when cold, wind and precipitation can become materially more important for outdoor games. Those are exactly the three weeks that decide the $900 prize total.

Weather suppresses passing more than running: wind ruins deep throws, cold and rain raise the odds of a dropped ball, and teams respond by handing it off and shortening the game. The practical consequence is that outdoor cold-weather passing games get quieter in the exact weeks you need ceiling, while indoor and warm-weather offenses keep doing what they do. Kickers are hit hardest of anyone — and in this league every missed kick is minus one point.

This is a real consideration and a small one, and it is easy to overcook it in July. Weather and the fantasy playoffs is where it gets quantified rather than gestured at.
