How the league is won

The money is paid to three teams out of twelve, in two stages, and the shape of that payout decides how the roster should be built.

The payout table

Finish Prize Net of the $75 stake
1st $500 +$425
2nd $250 +$175
3rd $150 +$75
4th – 12th $0 −$75

Confirmed from the league's own settings on 2026-07-25. The $100 buy-in splits into $75 into the prize pool and a $25 participation deposit that is refundable. Twelve teams times $75 is $900, and $500 plus $250 plus $150 is $900 — the pool balances exactly, so the expected return on a randomly played season is precisely zero. Any edge is pure profit, and any drift is pure loss.

Finishing 4th pays what finishing 12th pays

That is the sentence to keep. Fourth place is a season of attention, and it is worth the same as never opening the app: nothing. Nine of twelve teams lose the identical $75. There is no partial credit, no consolation, no sliding scale.

And at the other end, first place is worth more than second and third combined — $500 against $400.

So the payoff is flat along most of its range and then rises steeply at the very end. That shape has a name — it is convex — and convex payoffs reward variance.

Here is the reason in plain terms, and it is the same reason a call option is worth more than the expected value of the thing it tracks. When the downside is capped and the upside is not, outcomes that are merely bad and outcomes that are catastrophically bad cost you the same amount. Losing by a lot is not worse than losing by a little. But winning by a lot is much better than winning by a little. Anything that widens the spread of possible seasons therefore adds value at the top without adding any cost at the bottom, because the bottom is already floored at −$75.

The practical translation: a roster that is reliably decent is a roster built to finish 5th, which is built to lose $75. A roster with a real chance of being the best in the league — and a matching chance of being one of the worst — is the one worth owning. See Convex payoffs and roster variance for the concept on its own.

Where "build for variance" becomes bad advice

If the sentence stopped there it would be wrong, because pure variance never reaches the money at all. The season is two stages, and they reward different things.

Weeks 1 through 14 — finish top 6 of 12. This is a fourteen-week accumulation of head-to-head results, and the thing that survives fourteen weeks is a high average. A team that scores well most weeks qualifies. A boom-or-bust team that wins four games 160–90 and loses ten games 85–110 does not, no matter how spectacular its four wins were. Consistency cannot be punted here or the season is over before the money exists.

Weeks 15 through 17 — win a six-team bracket. Now everything reverses. There is no accumulation left. The task is to beat one specific opponent in one specific week, three times, with $500 at the end. Average scoring stops mattering the moment you have qualified; what matters is the size of the good weeks. A team that scores 145 on its best day and 95 on its worst beats a team that scores 118 every week roughly half the time. Winning the bracket means doing that three weeks running, so the high-variance team takes the whole thing about one time in eight. That is more than a steady team ever gets, because the steady team's best week is not big enough to beat anybody's best week.

So: the regular season buys the ticket, the playoffs cash it. The mean gets you in. The ceiling wins it.

The resolution, in draft terms

Two rules, and they do not conflict because they apply to different parts of the draft.

Do not sacrifice the mean in rounds 1 through 4. Those four picks are buying the playoff berth. They are the players who start every week for fourteen weeks and are the largest single input into whether the average is high enough to finish top six. This is the wrong place to be clever, to chase a player whose outcome is a wide distribution, or to reach for something the market does not price. Take the reliable, high-volume players. See How much to spend on running backs for the specific version of this argument.

Prefer upside from round 7 onward. By round 7 the reliable players are gone and what is left is priced on expectation, which means ceiling is cheap. A bench player who can be trusted for a safe 12 points a week is the least valuable thing on the roster, because 12 points a week is exactly what the waiver wire supplies for free and it will never win a playoff game. The bench exists to hold lottery tickets, not to hold spare parts.

The schedule is random, so your record is a noisy signal

The six playoff berths come off a randomly generated schedule. Every week you are matched against one of the other eleven teams, and which one is luck. It is entirely possible to be the third-highest-scoring team in the league and miss the playoffs, because your opponents happened to post their best weeks against you.

This matters for one reason above all: a 6–8 record is not proof the draft failed. In a fourteen-game season with one opponent a week, the gap between points scored and games won is large and it does not close. Before concluding anything went wrong, look at total points scored and where that ranks. If it is top six and the record is not, nothing was diagnosed — that was the schedule. Adjusting a good process because of a bad record is how a second season goes worse than a first.

The corollary runs the other way too: a 9–5 record built on the sixth-most points in the league is not evidence that anything is working.

The $25 that has nothing to do with winning

The $25 participation deposit is refunded for participation only — it is forfeited to first place if the league judges you inactive. It is not a prize and it is not competitive. It is a fee for showing up.

Which is why the weekly routine stays alive even in a season that is mathematically dead by Week 10. Setting a lineup takes a few minutes; not setting it is a $25 penalty on top of the $75 already lost, and it is the one part of this league where effort and reward connect directly. The fantasy football week is the checklist.

Open questions

  • The claim that a high-variance team wins a three-week bracket more often than a steady one is directionally right but the magnitude is not measured here, and it depends on the actual spread of weekly scores in this specific league, which will not be observable until several weeks in.
  • "Do not sacrifice mean in rounds 1–4, prefer upside from round 7" leaves rounds 5 and 6 deliberately unspecified. That is the transition zone and the honest answer is that it depends on what the board looks like — see Drafting from the 1.01.
  • Whether the league actually enforces the inactivity forfeit, and what standard it uses, is not written down anywhere in the settings. It is being treated as real.
