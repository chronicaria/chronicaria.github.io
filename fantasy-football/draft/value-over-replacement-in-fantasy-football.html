Value over replacement in fantasy football

A player is worth what he scores minus what you could have had free at his position — the one idea that turns a list of point totals into a draft board.

You already own this idea

In fantasy basketball you never compare two players by raw production. You compare each one to what the waiver wire would hand you for nothing at that role, and you draft the gap. That gap is the whole point. A player who beats his replacement by a lot is worth a pick; a player who beats his replacement by a little is worth a shrug, however big his box score looks.

Value over replacement — VOR (glossary) — is that same subtraction. A player's projected points, minus the points freely available at his position. Nothing more.

What changes in football is not the idea. It is that the subtraction matters far more here, and that the amount you subtract differs enormously from position to position. In basketball your instinct is calibrated by categories and by flexible position eligibility. Here, positions are rigid and the lineup is a cage, so raw points across positions are not merely a rough guide — they are actively meaningless.

Why raw points lie

Your lineup starts one quarterback, two running backs, two receivers, one tight end, one FLEX (a slot that accepts a RB, WR or TE), one kicker and one defence. Every week. Regardless of who is any good.

That means a quarterback's 366 projected points do not compete with a running back's 367 projected points. They compete with the other quarterback you would have started instead, because the quarterback slot is getting filled either way. The only thing a pick can buy you is the difference between the player you took and the player you would have had anyway.

Two structural facts drive everything that follows.

One: demand is fixed by the roster. Twelve teams times one quarterback slot is twelve starting quarterbacks, and the same for tight end, kicker and defence. Nobody can decide to start three quarterbacks because quarterbacks score a lot. The demand at those positions cannot flex by a single slot all season.

Two: supply is wildly uneven. All 32 NFL teams field roughly one starting quarterback and roughly one lead running back — but roughly three usable receivers each, because an offense throws to several people and hands off to mostly one. So the league produces something like 32 startable quarterbacks against 12 slots, and close to 100 startable receivers against 31. Fixed demand, unequal supply. That is the entire engine. See Positional scarcity as a supply constraint.

The second column answers a different and narrower question — the edge over the 12th-best player at the position, one per team. For tight end and quarterback the two are the same number, because only twelve of each ever get started. For running back and receiver the first column is the larger one, and it is the one that matters, because the FLEX means more than twelve of each are started every week.

Read the first column as: locking up the top running back is worth about eleven points a week against the last back you could still start, while locking up the top quarterback is worth about four. Weekly margins in this league are routinely under ten points, so the running back number decides games and the quarterback number mostly does not.

The quarterback paradox

Quarterbacks score the most raw points of anyone. They also go last in every well-run draft. Both are true, and VOR is why.

The best quarterback on the board is a rushing QB projected for 366 points — essentially identical to the best running back's 367. But the 12th quarterback and the 13th quarterback both project to 291. There is no gap to buy. Spending an early pick to get 366 instead of 291 buys 4.4 points a week; spending it on a three-down bell cow buys 10.7.

Points do not equal value. That sentence is the beginner's entire fix. See Late-round quarterback.

The defence and kicker rows are model outputs, not observations

The D/ST figure of 3.4 per week over the 12th-best defence is roughly three times the 1.1 per week that _PLAN.md §5.2 guessed before the build ran — its own pre-build estimate, flagged there as "verify before use", not a figure taken from any published ranking. That disagreement is deliberate rather than an error.

This league runs active yards-allowed brackets — a defence gains or loses points based on total yards surrendered, on top of points allowed. Virtually no published defence ranking prices that, because most leagues do not use it. So the defence numbers on the board are computed from projected yards and points allowed under this league's own scale.

Say it plainly: that number is modelled, not observed. Treat it as an ordering of defences, not as a forecast of their scores.

The kicker row is modelled in exactly the same sense. The source projection gives makes and attempts with no distances, so this league's 3/4/5/6-by-distance scale is applied through an assumed league-average distance mix — meaning every made field goal is valued identically. Read the kicker figures as an ordering too, never as a forecast.

Note also that the board deliberately baselines kicker and defence at the 3rd-ranked player rather than the 12th, which is why their first column above is so small. Those are the only two positions the waiver wire reliably restocks every single week, so what is "freely available" there is close to the best streamable option, not the 12th-best season-long name. Their positional edge — the second column — is real; their draft value is nearly nothing. See The last two rounds — kicker and defence and Streaming a position.

What VOR does not price — and what Adj VOR now does

Plain VOR compares totals as if everyone plays all seventeen games. Earlier versions of this note added a second claim on top of that — that running back is the most injury-prone position, so the running-back-heavy top of the board was partly an artefact. The first half is still true. The second half was an assumption, and measured, it does not hold.

Across 2023–2025, among the players on this board with three full seasons behind them, the share of seasons lost to four or more missed games is RB 27% · WR 24% · QB 29% · TE 32%, and the share of near-lost seasons — eight games or fewer — is RB 6% · WR 8% · QB 16% · TE 6%. Running backs do not miss four-plus-game stretches more often than quarterbacks or tight ends, and their rate of near-lost seasons is the joint lowest of the four positions. Receivers miss at least one game more often than backs do, 67% of seasons against 63%. These are observed games played, read out of Data/player_context.csv (nflverse, retrieved 2026-07-25); the full table is in Availability and injury risk.

On none of those four measures is running back the worst position, and the position that is consistently worst is quarterback — lowest mean availability of the four, and the highest rate of near-lost seasons by more than double. What is left between backs and receivers is marginal: the median back and the median receiver each missed 6 of a possible 51 games over those three years, and receivers hold a mean availability of 0.861 against the backs' 0.857. A back is not more likely to miss games than a receiver. The fragile position, measured, is quarterback. Two limits, held honestly — this pool is defined by 2026 projection, so it is survivor-selected, and a back whose career was ended by injury is not in it to be counted; and three seasons is a thin sample for a rate.

The board's Adj VOR column is the answer to the objection this note used to have to concede. It is a model, not an observation. It scales each player's projection by his availability relative to his position's mean — his Avail divided by the mean Avail at his position — and recomputes value over replacement on the scaled numbers. Dividing by the position mean is the whole point: a published projection already prices the average player's risk of missing time, so multiplying by raw availability would charge for it twice. An average-durability player does not move at all; only players away from their position's norm do. Avail is itself a model too — a three-year games-played rate shrunk toward the position average, so one clean rookie season does not read as proof of iron. Read Adj VOR as an ordering of durability-adjusted value, never as a forecast: it prices what a player has done, not what he will do.

Where the baselines came from

They were derived from this league, not copied from a published source's assumed lineup.

Twelve teams times the starting lineup gives the fixed slot counts, plus 12 FLEX slots to hand out. In full PPR (every catch is one point) the FLEX splits roughly 40% running back, 55% receiver and 5% tight end, because catches make pass-catching backs and slot receivers startable.

Position Fixed slots (12 teams) Share of the 12 FLEX slots Effective starters — the baseline
RB 24 40% ≈ 5 29
WR 24 55% ≈ 7 31
TE 12 5% ≈ 0 12
QB 12 none 12

That last column is why the running back and receiver baselines sit past 24 rather than at it, and why only those two positions have a replacement figure different from their 12th-best figure.

Open questions

  • The FLEX split of 40/55/5 is an assumption, not a measurement. It moves the running back and receiver baselines by two or three ranks each, which shifts VOR by a few points but does not reorder the positions.
  • The projections underneath still assume 17 games from everybody, so plain VOR is a healthy-season figure. Adj VOR corrects for that with a three-year availability rate, which prices what has already happened and not what happens this September.
  • The 3.4 defence figure has the right direction but an unverified magnitude — no published ranking prices the yards-allowed brackets, so there is nothing external to check it against.
