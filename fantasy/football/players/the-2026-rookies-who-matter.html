The 2026 rookies who matter

Why a football rookie is different

In basketball, a rookie earns minutes over two or three seasons, and a first-year player who matters in fantasy is a rare event. In football the job is assigned, not earned. Roles are handed out by a coaching staff before week 1, and a team that spends a high pick on a running back has publicly committed to using him — the pick is the plan, and the plan is usually executed immediately. A back drafted third overall can carry the ball twenty times in his first professional game.

So your basketball instinct here is a false friend: "wait a year on rookies" is correct in one sport and expensive in the other. The reason this note exists is that you have no prior on any of these names, and your leaguemates who watched college football do. Draft capital dominance is the concept underneath it — where a player was drafted predicts his workload better than anything he does on the field, because the team that spent the pick has an incentive to justify it.

How I established rookie status

I did not work from memory. I pulled the full 2026 NFL draft results (all 257 picks, rounds 1–7) from published draft trackers, then intersected that list against the promoted Data/players.csv board. Anyone I could not match to a confirmed 2026 draft pick is not in this note, including undrafted rookies who may well make rosters — the file gives no rookie flag, so an undrafted first-year player is invisible to this method. I have not invented a college statistic, a draft position, or a combine number anywhere below.

Two labels apply to every line that follows. ADP is average draft position — the average slot a player goes at across thousands of practice drafts, i.e. his market price (see ADP as a market price and the Fantasy football glossary). The current gated values are Fantasy Football Calculator 10-team PPR proxies dated 2026-08-02, not ESPN ADP. Every points total remains a forecast; nothing below has happened.

Archetype strings are quoted verbatim from the archetype column — the closed set defined in Player archetypes.

Andrew drafts first in a 10-team snake: the relevant paired turns are 20/21, 40/41, 60/61, 80/81, 100/101, 120/121, and 140/141. ADP is not a promise that a player reaches any of them.

The backs

Jeremiyah Love (Arizona, drafted 3rd overall). three-down bell cow; ADP 24.2, 259.9 projected points, 246 carries and 56 catches. The receiving component is valuable in full PPR, but it is still a role projection with no NFL usage behind it. His ADP sits just after Andrew's 20/21 turn: do not assume he reaches it, and use only the current 10-team geometry.

Jadarian Price (Seattle, drafted 32nd overall). open backfield competition; ADP 65.8, 185.3 projected points, 195 carries and 31 catches, risk high. Seattle identifies an open camp competition with George Holani and Kobe Wilson, while Zach Charbonnet's return timing remains unresolved. Treat the projection as a conditional workload, not a starting-role confirmation; the price falls between Andrew's 60/61 and 80/81 turns.

Mike Washington Jr. (Las Vegas, drafted 122nd overall). handcuff; ADP 141.0, 57.6 projected points, 85 carries and 7 catches, risk high. His current price is at Andrew's 140/141 turn, but the role is conditional on a change ahead of him. That makes him an upside bench choice, not a player to force into the turn.

The receivers

Six of the eleven are wideouts, which is what happens when a draft class is thin at running back and deep at receiver. Note that five of the six carry the same archetype:

Carnell Tate (Tennessee, 4th overall). big slot; ADP 73.3, 181.7 projected points, 65 catches for 867 yards, risk med. The catch volume is useful in full PPR, but it remains a rookie receiving-role projection. His ADP falls between the 60/61 and 80/81 turns.

Jordyn Tyson (New Orleans, 8th overall). big slot; ADP 82.8, 163.3 projected points, 61 catches for 783 yards, risk med. The model gives him a meaningful reception base, but the role is still unconfirmed; he is a post-80/81 target, not a reason to reach early.

KC Concepcion (Cleveland, 24th overall). big slot; ADP 126.3, 153.6 projected points, 58 catches for 752 yards, risk low. The model's risk flag is not confirmation of a rookie's target share. His price comes after the 120/121 turn; use depth-chart evidence to decide whether the projection is actionable.

Makai Lemon (Philadelphia, 20th overall). big slot; ADP 101.6, 147.7 projected points, 47 catches for 558 yards, risk med. Philadelphia's A.J. Brown trade and a new offense leave target allocation unresolved. The current price is essentially the 100/101 turn, and the uncertainty is the reason not to treat the model as settled volume.

Omar Cooper Jr. (New York Jets, 30th overall). big slot; ADP 154.1, 107.6 projected points, 37 catches for 466 yards, risk med. His rookie role remains unconfirmed. He is later than the 140/141 turn, so he is a watch-list or final-bench decision rather than a core draft target.

Denzel Boston (Cleveland, 39th overall). big slot; ADP 151.3, 104.6 projected points, 40 catches for 526 yards, risk med. He shares an offense with Concepcion, so both projections cannot be treated as independent upside. The role remains contested and he is later than Andrew's 140/141 turn.

The quarterback and the kicker

Fernando Mendoza (Las Vegas, 1st overall). He is deliberately absent from the promoted board: Kirk Cousins opened camp as QB1, so there is no current ADP or 17-game starter projection to use. Do not draft him from the former numerical baseline. Reconsider only after an official starting-order reversal and supporting preseason work.

Trey Smack (Green Bay, 216th overall). 142.0 projected points and no ADP; the number is a modelled kicker estimate, not a projected stat line. Stream kicker by Implied team total, not by name. A rookie competing for a job is not a draftable conclusion.

The only August instruction: ignore preseason box scores

Preseason games are played in August and the statistics from them are actively misleading. Starters play one or two possessions and then sit; the leaders in preseason rushing yards are, almost every year, players who will be cut before week 1. A rookie running up 90 yards against fourth-string defenders has told you nothing.

Two things from August do matter, and neither is a box score:

  1. Depth-chart position — where the coaching staff lists him, and who is ahead of him. Reading an NFL depth chart covers how to read one without being fooled by it.
  2. Injury news — specifically injuries to the player ahead of him, which is how most rookies get promoted.

And one date dominates: the August 30 roster cutdown, when every team must reduce to its final roster. That is the largest single depth-chart resolution of the year, it happens in a day, and it reprices dozens of the players above — several of the high risk flags in this note exist precisely because the file was built before it. If your league draft falls after August 30, this note should be rebuilt first.

Open questions

  • Confidence in the identifications is high but not complete. The listed rookies are confirmed against published 2026 draft results and matched by name to the promoted board where a current record is eligible. The gap runs the other way: undrafted 2026 rookies are undetectable by this method, so the list may be short by one or two — most likely a receiver or back on a thin depth chart.
  • Name matching is exact-string, so a rookie entered in the file under a different spelling or a suffix difference would have been silently dropped.
  • Six listed receivers carry the identical big slot label, which is more likely a limitation of applying a closed archetype set to players with no professional snaps than a real fact about six different offenses.
