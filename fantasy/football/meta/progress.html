Decisions taken during the build

Kicker and D/ST replacement baselines are set at rank 3, not 12. These are the only two positions where the wire reliably offers a startable option every week, so "the 12th best season-long" is the wrong replacement. Baselining them at 12 put Houston's defence at board rank 28, which is nonsense. Every other position uses the _PLAN §1.2 derived starter count (RB 29, WR 31, TE 12, QB 12). The board's baseline table still prints the rank-12 comparison, because that is the number Value over replacement in fantasy football argues from.

D/ST projected points are modelled and labelled as such. No published ranking prices this league's active yards-allowed brackets (_PLAN §5.2a), so the bracket contribution is computed from projected points and yards allowed under a normal approximation, not read off a source. The board says so in a callout. The result — a 3.4 point-per-week gap between the best defence and a typical starter, against the ~1.1 that published rankings imply — is exactly the understatement §5.2a predicted, but it is a model output, not an observation.

Kicker points use a modelled field-goal distance mix. Projections give makes and attempts, not distances, and this league pays 3/4/5/6 by distance with −1 for a miss. The distance split is the league-wide attempt mix, so kicker totals are ordering, not forecasts.

The turn map excludes K and D/ST. They are taken in the last two rounds by rule, not because value says so.

ADP is a labelled proxy. ESPN's own ADP could not be extracted programmatically (_PLAN §10.5 #2). Every row carries adp_source. ESPN mocks are the substitute measurement, which is another reason mocks are on the critical path.

Findings that correct _PLAN.md

Each was found by a writer checking a plan assertion against players.csv rather than repeating it. Verified independently before being applied, per §7.

  1. "Week 6 second worst" is not what the data says. _PLAN §5.1 #7 calls week 11 the crunch (six teams off — confirmed) and week 6 second worst. In players.csv, weeks 6, 7, 8, 10 and 13 are all tied at four teams; weeks 5, 9 and 14 have two; week 12 has none. Bye-week planning and The 2026 season calendar state the tie accurately and argue week 6 is the worst of the four-team weeks because it is the earliest, which follows from the build's own "early byes hurt more" rule rather than from a miscount.
  2. The elite tight ends are not gone before pick 24. _PLAN §5.2 #4 says the elite TE tier goes "~20–25", which would lock the 1.01 out of it entirely. The board says 28.9 and 35.8 — both just past picks 24 and 25. The honest version, now in Drafting from the 1.01: the 1.01 gets exactly one shot at the tier, at 24/25, and taking it spends half of the best turn on the position with the smallest per-week edge. By 48/49 both are gone.
  3. _PLAN.md never specifies this league's per-event D/ST values. §1.1 and §5.2a give the yards-allowed brackets, one points-allowed value (0 allowed = 5) and safety = 4, but no sack, interception or fumble-recovery value. build_board.py assumes the ESPN defaults — sack 1, interception 2, fumble recovery 2, defensive or return touchdown 6 — and this is an unverified assumption, not a confirmed setting. Read the rest off ESPN before the draft.
  4. players.csv is structurally silent on availability. Every row carries games: 17 and there is no injury field, so no projection in this build is discounted for missed time. The risk column is a projection-confidence flag, not a durability rating. Player notes say so rather than implying otherwise.
  5. Two _PLAN claims about specific players could not be verified and were not asserted. The "Bo Nix finished top-8 in total quarterback points but roughly QB10 per game" claim has no source in this build, and no candidate's injury history could be checked. Both are flagged in the notes as unverified rather than silently converted to fact.

The join bug a validator caught

build_context.py carries a validator whose only job is to refute its own join: if a player's projection line calls for 40 or more catches, or 150 or more carries, and his joined 2025 season shows fewer than 10 receptions or 20 carries, the match is almost certainly a different human.

It fired on D.J. Moore — a big slot — matched to David Moore, an unrelated receiver who is not even in players.csv. The name normaliser was splitting "D.J." into two tokens, so "d j moore" reduced to a Moore-plus-initial that David Moore also satisfied. D.J. Moore had been carrying David Moore's three-year games played, which would have flowed straight into his avail and from there into his adjusted board rank. Fixed by joining runs of single letters before matching.

The point worth recording: the defect was found by a check written to fail, not by reading the output. Nothing in the finished CSV looked wrong.

The finding that contradicts _PLAN §10.5 risk 4

§10.5 risk 4 asserts that VOR does not price running-back injury risk "and RBs are the most injury-prone position — roughly 7 in 10 lead backs miss at least one game", then uses that to caveat the running-back-heavy top of the board. Measured over the 134 board players who have three full seasons of history:

Pos Players Seasons missing 1+ game Missing 4+ Eight games or fewer
QB 24 68% 32% 19%
RB 41 65% 29% 8%
WR 46 67% 24% 8%
TE 23 70% 32% 6%

The "7 in 10" rate is right in magnitude and wrong in attribution. Every position sits between 65% and 70%, and running back is the lowest of the four; backs also lose whole seasons less often than quarterbacks or tight ends. The caveat that survives is a shape claim, not a rate claim — running back carries the fatter left tail, so backs are not more likely to miss a game, they are more likely to miss a lot of them. Availability and injury risk states it that way, and The injury-inheritance asymmetry now says which half of its own claim this grounds and which half it does not.

One limit, stated on the board and in the note: this pool is the top ~200 by 2026 projection, so it is survivor-selected. A career ended by injury is not in it. The narrow claim — among the players you would actually draft, running-back availability is not measurably worse than receiver availability — holds. The wider claim that the position destroys careers faster is untested here and may well be true.

The third and fourth deliberately-labelled models

Pass 1 shipped two model outputs and labelled both: D/ST projected points, and the kicker field-goal distance mix. This pass adds two more. The running count is worth keeping, because a model that quietly stops being labelled is how a build starts lying.

  1. avail — a player's three-year games-played rate shrunk toward his position's base rate by the weight of one full season (PRIOR_W = 17). Without the shrinkage a rookie at 17-of-17 scores a perfect 1.00 off a single season, which is the exact small-sample error Sample size in a 17-game season warns against everywhere else; that note now uses this as its worked example. Players with no NFL history carry the position base rate outright and are flagged avail_basis = position base rate.
  2. Adj VOR — value over replacement recomputed after scaling each projection by avail divided by the position's mean avail. The scaling is deliberately relative: a published projection already prices the average player's injury risk, so multiplying by raw availability would count it twice. An average-durability player does not move at all.

Everything else in player_context.csv is observed and is presented as such. Games played, injury-report weeks, age, draft position and contract terms are counted facts; avail and Adj VOR are marked as models on every surface they appear on.

One reading caution now baked into the notes: a missed game is not automatically an injury. games_missed_3yr counts absence of every kind, and missed_off_injury_report shows that most missed games in this file were not accompanied by a week-of listing on the official report — partly because a player placed on injured reserve drops off the weekly report entirely, and partly because suspensions, holdouts and inactive backups all read the same way in a games-played column. The two fields are kept separate rather than merged for this reason.

Pass 1b — the context layer and the website (2026-07-25)

Andrew asked for two things the first pass could not do: real injury, age, contract and usage history for every player, and the whole vault rendered as a website.

The context layer

Data/player_context.csv — 203 skill players, observed, joined from nflverse (2026 rosters, 2023–2025 season stat lines, 2023–2025 official weekly injury reports, snap counts) and OverTheCap's live contract pages. Run order, and the only ordering constraint in the build:

./fetch_nflverse.sh → build_context.py → build_board.py → simulate_season.py

build_board.py fails closed if the context file is missing. nflverse's own contracts release was unusable — it stops at year_signed 2022, so it has nothing for anyone drafted since — hence OverTheCap.

Three data defects, all found by checks rather than by reading

  1. A wrong-player join. D.J. Moore matched David Moore, inheriting 27 missed games instead of 0. Caught by adding a validator that flags any joined row whose observed usage is impossible given the projection — a receiver projected for 71 catches cannot have caught 1 last season. It now runs on every build and currently reports zero.
  2. A missing input silently becoming a zero — the exact failure _PLAN.md §6 forbids. Five players had no stat line found for seasons they played, and the code scored that as seventeen missed games: Kenny Gainwell came out at 0.197 availability after three healthy seasons. Nickname forms now match, and a season with no stat line is excluded from the denominator and recorded in seasons_unmatched rather than counted as missed.
  3. Positional column assignment. Games played were written by right-aligning a list, so a player missing one season had his 2024 total printed under 2025. Now keyed by season.

The RB-injury finding, and how it changed under correction

The first version of this claimed running backs were more durable than receivers. After defects 1–3 were fixed the gap closed almost to nothing, and the honest version is narrower:

Pos Mean availability Missing 1+ Missing 4+ Eight or fewer
QB 0.806 67% 29% 16%
RB 0.857 63% 27% 6%
WR 0.861 67% 24% 8%
TE 0.830 70% 32% 6%

On none of the four measures is running back the worst position, and quarterback is worst on two. So _PLAN.md §10.5 risk 4 — "RBs are the most injury-prone position" — is not what the record shows, and the board's caveat has been rewritten. The pool is survivor-selected (players who mattered in 2026 and were in the league all three years), which is stated in the note.

The comparative sentences in that note are now derived from its own table, not asserted. A verification pass caught the previous version claiming running backs had "the lowest rate of near-lost seasons" three lines below a table showing they did not. Prose that restates a computed table will eventually contradict it; prose generated from the table cannot.

Availability is not injury

Avail counts a missed game whatever caused it, so suspensions, holdouts and healthy backups all land in it. The note now prints the players whose absences mostly never reached an official injury report — Rashee Rice's 23 missed games include only 2 weeks listed Out — so the number is never read as fragility when it is not.

Two new models, and one measurement

  • Adj VOR scales each projection by the player's availability relative to his position's mean, then recomputes value over replacement. Relative on purpose: a published projection already prices the average player's risk, so scaling by raw availability would count it twice. This closes the caveat the first board had to concede.
  • When to start drafting for upside finally measures _PLAN.md's open question. The answer is that the question was posed wrong — see the note. Two versions of the experiment had to be thrown away first: one where "upside" and "value" produced identical rosters from round 7 on, so the sweep measured nothing, and one where the drafter paid for role upside the simulated season never delivered.

§9.4 — the post-draft decision, written down now

  • The perishable research layer is abandoned at the draft. Board, tiers, ADP deltas, turn map, player notes, rookie note. A half-maintained board that still looks authoritative in Week 6 is worse than no board.
  • The weekly checklist stays alive to the final whistle. Tuesday claims → Wednesday adds → Friday designations → Sunday inactives → start/sit by implied team total. One page: The fantasy football week and Start-sit — a standing rule, not a research project. The $25 deposit is refunded only for participation, so going quiet in Week 8 costs real money on top of the lost season.
