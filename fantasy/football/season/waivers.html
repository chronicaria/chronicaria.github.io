The Season

Waivers

Your waiver claim is use-it-or-lose-it, because this league recomputes the order from scratch every week.

as of 2026-08-02 · status seed · format espn-ppr1.0-10tm-std

The setting that changes everything

A waiver is a holding period on a dropped or unowned player: instead of first-come-first-served, everyone submits claims and they are processed in a fixed order overnight. The question is always how that order is set.

Most leagues use rolling priority: you start at some position, and if you win a claim you drop to the bottom and stay there until you win another. Priority is a real, storable asset — you hold it, you spend it once, you choose when.

This league does not do that. The waiver order resets every week to the inverse of the current standings (confirmed 2026-07-25). Last place claims first, first place claims last, and last week's spending has nothing to do with it. Three consequences follow, and none of them is obvious.

1. An unspent claim expires worthless

There is no option value in saving your priority for a better week. Next week's order is recomputed from your win-loss record regardless of whether you claimed anyone. Sitting out a week buys you exactly nothing.

This is a false friend from fantasy basketball, and it is worth flagging as such. In a rolling-priority or FAAB league, restraint is a strategy: not spending is itself a position, and "I'm saving my number one claim" is a coherent sentence. You already own that model, it feels like sophistication, and here it is simply wrong. Ported over, it makes you pass on marginal-but-real upgrades all season while the value you were "saving" evaporates every Tuesday night.

The correct default is the opposite: if this week's best available player is better than your worst bench player, claim him. The claim is a coupon with an expiry date on it, not a bank balance. See What basketball teaches you wrong about football for the rest of the transfer errors.

2. Winning degrades your waiver access

If you are in first place, you claim tenth every week. Not once as a penalty for a successful claim — permanently, for as long as you keep winning. The better your team does, the less the wire can help it.

Two practical consequences. First, the draft matters more than it would elsewhere, because the mechanism that normally lets a good team keep improving is switched off for good teams specifically. Whatever you do not draft, you may not be able to buy later. Second, early-season aggression is worth more than late-season aggression. In weeks 1–3 the standings have not sorted yet and everybody's priority is roughly arbitrary; that is the window where you have the same access as anyone. By week 8, if the plan has worked, you are claiming last. Spend hard early.

3. Losing teams get first crack at every breakout, every week

The system is deliberately compressive — it is designed to hand the biggest new asset to whoever needs it most. Every week, the team in tenth gets the first look at whatever emerged on Sunday.

Which means: you cannot count on winning a contested claim. Any player obvious enough that four teams want him will go to whichever of those four has the worst record. Plan around this rather than resenting it. Your realistic wins on waivers are (a) the players nobody else has noticed yet, and (b) everything that happens after waivers clear.

The Wednesday free-for-all is the real second market

The waiver period is 1 day and there is no acquisition limit. Claims process overnight into Wednesday morning, and at that moment every player nobody claimed stops being on waivers and becomes a plain free agent — first-come-first-served, no order, no priority.

This is where a contending team actually competes, because speed and attention replace standings position as the currency. Two habits follow:

  • Check Wednesday morning, every week. Not because the best player is there, but because the second-best player usually is, and nobody's record protects him.
  • Submit the claim anyway on Tuesday. With no acquisition limit and nothing to conserve, there is no cost to trying for the contested player and then taking the uncontested one on Wednesday.

Ten rosters leave more viable players unrostered than a 12-team league would. That raises the floor of Wednesday replacements and makes streaming more credible, especially for one-week injuries, byes, kicker, and D/ST. It does not mean the rare new starter will be free: a true role-changing injury is still a league-wide claim, and the inverse-standings order still decides it. With no keepers, there is also no next-year value to protect; use the deeper pool to cut a speculative bench stash when a current-season contributor is available.

Why you watch the wire at all: injury inheritance

Here is the single biggest reason any of this matters, and it is the sharpest structural difference between the two sports.

When an NBA star goes down, his roughly 35 minutes get redistributed across three or four teammates. Everybody gets a modest bump, nobody becomes a star, and you spend a week working out which of the four got the most.

Football does not divide the role. It transfers it. When a lead back is injured, one teammate typically absorbs 80–90% of the carries and the goal-line work — the handoffs inside the five-yard line, where touchdowns are worth six points against a yard's 0.1 — essentially overnight. A player who was unrosterable on Sunday afternoon is a weekly starter on Wednesday, at a waiver price, because in this sport the touches belong to the position on the depth chart rather than to the man.

The archetype vocabulary makes this concrete. A handcuff is the backup to a three-down bell cow, and he is worth nothing at all until the moment he is worth a second-round pick. There is no gradual version. See Player archetypes and, for the full mechanics and what it implies at the draft, The injury-inheritance asymmetry.

So the wire is not mostly about finding a slightly better bench receiver. It is about being present on the one or two Tuesdays a season when an entire starting role changes hands — and, given consequence 3 above, about being far enough down the standings, or fast enough on Wednesday, to actually get him.

Open questions

  • The 80–90% inheritance figure is a general football pattern, not something measured from 2026 data in this vault. Some backfields split the vacated role genuinely evenly.
  • Whether the weekly reset uses the standings as of Tuesday processing or as of the previous week's completion is unconfirmed; it only matters in the rare week where a Monday result flips the order.
  • "Claim if better than your worst bench player" ignores bye-week coverage, which can make a worse player more useful. See Bye-week planning.
