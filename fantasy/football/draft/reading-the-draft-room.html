The Draft

Reading the draft room

A draft is a live adversarial event against eleven other people, not a list you read down — and what the room does between your picks decides what is left when you get back.

The blind window is the problem

From the 1.01 you pick first, and then twenty-two players come off the board before you pick again at 24. That is the largest gap any team in the league faces, and it happens once per turn all draft long (the turn).

You cannot control those 22 picks. You can predict them, and the prediction is not "the 22 highest-ranked players will go." It is "the 22 players that this room's momentum points at will go." Momentum has a name.

Positional runs, and why football runs are sharper

A positional run is several managers taking the same position back to back, because the scarcity became visible to everyone at once. Someone takes the second-to-last tight end in a tier, three other managers look at what is left, and the rest of the tier is gone within six picks.

In fantasy basketball a run is annoying. In football it is decisive, and the reason is structural: football tiers are two or three players deep. Look at Tiers by position, rendered from Data/players.csv on 2026-07-25 — the top running back tier holds three names. The third overall tier holds five. When a group that small starts moving, waiting one turn does not cost you a slightly worse player; it costs you the entire class of player.

Compare quarterback, where the top tier on that same page runs fourteen names deep. A quarterback run cannot hurt you, because the fourteenth name in the tier is worth nearly what the first is. Tight end is the opposite: the top tier is seven names and then the value falls off a cliff. So the general rule has a precise form — a run only matters where the tier is shallow, and you can look up which those are before the draft starts.

What a run looks like on the ESPN draft screen

ESPN's live draft room gives you three things worth watching. (Run one mock draft on ESPN before draft day and confirm the layout yourself — this describes the room as of 2026-07-25.)

The pick log. A running list of every selection made, most recent first. This is your run detector. Do not read the names — you would not recognise most of them anyway. Read the position tags. Five of the last eight picks tagged TE is a tight end run in progress, and it will be over before your next turn.

The Best Available panel, filtered by position. The panel defaults to overall order. Click through to a single position and you can see the shape of what remains — this is where you check whether the tier you want has one name left or six. Have Tiers by position open beside it, because ESPN's own ordering is not this league's ordering.

The clock and the queue. You get 90 seconds. Your queue is a personal shortlist that also protects you: if the clock expires, ESPN picks for you, and a stocked queue means it picks something you chose. Keep it full at all times.

Seeing a run coming is mostly counting. Before your turn, ask one question: how many players are left in the tier I want, and how many picks until I am back? If the tier has two names and eleven picks separate you from your next selection, that tier is gone.

Read the other rosters — it is public information

ESPN shows you every team's roster during the draft, live. Almost nobody uses it. It answers the only question that actually predicts the next twenty picks: what does this room still need?

Twelve teams, one starting quarterback each, so twelve quarterbacks are needed all season. If you look at the roster view and count eight teams that already have one, then only four buyers remain and the position will not run. You can wait, comfortably, and take one at a turn where nothing else is worth taking. The same count on tight end tells you the opposite story faster, because the useful supply there is far shorter than twelve.

Do this once around picks 70–90 and once more late. It takes a minute and it converts a guess into a count.

When to jump a run, and when to let it pass

Decide this on tier depth, never on rank. Rank tells you who is better; tier depth tells you what the delay actually costs.

Jump the run when the tier you want has one or two players left and the drop to the next tier is real. This is the only legitimate reason to take a player "too early" — you are not reaching past his value, you are paying to stay inside a tier that is about to close. On the tight end board, the gap between the bottom of the top tier and the top of the next one is large enough that being one pick late means being a whole class late.

Let it pass when the tier below is nearly as good. If four managers panic and take quarterbacks in a row, let them. The fourteen-deep tier means the quarterback you get two rounds later is worth almost exactly what theirs is, and you spent your pick on a position where the gap is real. A run you sit out is a run that hands you value everywhere else on the board.

The failure mode is joining a run because it feels bad to watch one. That instinct is real and it is wrong. Ask what the tier looks like, not what the room is doing.

The 90-second clock

Ninety seconds is not enough time to think. It is barely enough time to decide between two things you already thought about.

So: before your turn arrives, have two names ready, not one. The first is your pick if he is there. The second is your pick if someone takes him in the intervening picks. Write them down. When the clock starts, you are executing, not deliberating.

This is where the 1.01 gives you an unusual advantage. At every turn you pick twice in a row — 24 and 25, 48 and 49, and so on to 168 and 169. Everyone else must choose between the player whose tier is closing and the safe player they actually want. You take both. Plan each turn as one decision about a pair, not two decisions about players: the tier-breaking pick and the solid one, together. See Drafting from the 1.01.

One corollary for the pair: do not take two players on the same bye week at the same turn without noticing. It is cheap to check and expensive to fix — see Bye-week planning.

The Week 1 trap

The 2026 season opens on Wednesday, September 9 (The 2026 season calendar). The draft date was still unconfirmed as of 2026-07-25, with late August expected.

If the draft lands on September 6, 7 or 8, your Week 1 lineup is effectively due at the draft. There is no waiver run between the two — claims here take a one-day waiting period, so nothing you want on the wire arrives in time. Whatever you walk away with is what you start.

Two consequences if the date falls there. First, your last few picks stop being pure lottery tickets and have to include a startable body at every position, because a hole in the roster on draft night is a zero in Week 1. Second, check injury designations before you take anyone in the final rounds — a player who will not play in Week 1 is a Week 1 bye you chose.

If the draft is in late August as expected, none of this binds: you get a full waiver cycle first and can draft purely for value. Confirm the date, then decide.
