1. Settings audit — do this before you look at a single player

Every row below changes a player's value. Read the actual league constitution, not the platform's marketing copy.

The two settings that change the most: scoring system and league size. Everything else is a modifier.

2. Two weeks out — a July board is not a draft board

This encyclopedia's board is stamped 2026-07-18. It was built before camp, before the schedule release, and before several transactions resolved. Treat it as a prior, not a plan.

Re-check in October, in this order:

  1. Roster finality. 2026-27 NBA roster and transaction ledger — the July board still carries open branches on Kawhi Leonard (trade held), Harden, Duren, and an unsigned LeBron James priced only on a conditional line.
  2. Medical clearance, not "cleared." The evidence bar in 2026-27 fantasy basketball evidence and confidence policy is unrestricted camp participation plus explicit back-to-back availability. Applies to Tatum, Haliburton, Anthony Davis, Embiid, and Brandon Miller.
  3. Preseason minutes and closing lineups. Not preseason box scores — minutes and who finishes games. This is what resolves Pritchard, Sheppard, Ausar Thompson, and Sharpe.
  4. The schedule. The full 2026-27 grid is expected mid-August 2026; opening night is reported as Tuesday, October 20, 2026, but the official NBA key-dates page had not confirmed it at this note's cutoff. NBA Cup group play (Oct 30–Nov 27) counts toward the 82 games; only the Dec 11 final does not (NBA Cup FAQ). Do not build a playoff-schedule plan before the grid exists.
  5. Platform position eligibility. Published at pre-rank release, not before. Multi-position eligibility is worth real value in leagues with rigid slots and is not knowable in July.
  6. Actual ADP. ADP and draft market desk found no defensible post-draft 2026-27 ADP as of July 18. Your edge is the gap between your board and the room's price — you cannot measure that gap until the price exists.

3. Draft from tiers, not from ranks

The reason is arithmetic, not philosophy. Using this vault's own points model (Data/player-projections-2026-07-18.csv, Yahoo-default weights):

Model rank Player FP/G Value over 10-team replacement (rank 130) Value over 12-team replacement (rank 156)
1 Jokić 61.4 +34.1 +36.5
12 Barnes 45.0 +17.7 +20.1
24 Trae Young 42.1 +14.8 +17.2
48 Knueppel 37.5 +10.2 +12.6
84 Keegan Murray 31.8 +4.6 +6.9
130 Melton 27.3 0.0 +2.4
156 Cam Johnson 24.9 −2.4 0.0

Two things fall out.

The board is not smooth. Ranks 1→12 cost \(61.4 - 45.0 = 16.4\) FP/G across eleven slots (\(1.49\) per slot). Ranks 12→84 cost \(45.0 - 31.8 = 13.2\) across seventy-two slots (\(0.18\) per slot). The first round is worth more than rounds 2 through 7 combined. Between ranks 24 and 84, one draft slot is worth about a tenth of what a slot in the top twelve is worth — which is exactly why treating adjacent ranks as meaningfully separated is a mistake, and why grouping them into tiers is not a simplification but a correction.

League size flips the emphasis. Elite-to-mid value ratio is \(34.1/10.2 = 3.34\) in a 10-team league versus \(36.5/12.6 = 2.90\) in a 12-team league. Ten-teamers should be more top-heavy, because a shallow league's replacement level is high enough that mid-round picks are nearly free — you can replace them from waivers. Twelve-teamers get relatively more from the middle rounds and should be less willing to punt depth for a star.

The tier-break rule

Let \(k\) = players left in the current tier and \(p\) = picks before your next turn. Let \(q\) = the share of opposing picks likely to come from that tier (roughly 0.25–0.4 for a broad tier, higher for a scarce-position tier).

\[\mathbb{E}[\text{survivors}] = k - pq\]

If $k - pq < 1$, the tier will not survive: take from it now, even if a lower tier contains a player you like more. If \(k - pq \ge 2\), take the best player from any tier and come back.

Worked: 12-team snake, you pick 4th, next turn is pick 21 — \(p = 16\). Five players left in the tier, \(q \approx 0.3\): \(\mathbb{E} = 5 - 4.8 = 0.2\). The tier is gone. Take one now. Same room, ten players left: \(\mathbb{E} = 10 - 4.8 = 5.2\). Wait, and take value elsewhere.

4. In-draft decision rules

Run these in order. Stop at the first one that fires.

  • IF a tier is about to break by the rule above AND it is a tier you need → take from it now.
  • IF two players are within one tier AND one fixes a category you are dead-last in → take the fixer. Rank order inside a tier is noise; category shape is not.
  • IF no tier is breaking → take best player available. "Need" is not a real constraint before roughly pick 60; you cannot know your holes yet.
  • IF you are in rounds 1–3 → do not draft two players with the same drain. Two −FT bigs in the first three rounds commits you to a punt you have not decided on.
  • IF you are past round 6 → only draft for a specific function: a scarce category, a minutes path, or an injury contingency. A "best available" pick in round 10 is a wasted slot.
  • IF a player's value depends on an unresolved branch (Tx, Rk, H! in 2026-27 fantasy basketball tiers) → require a discount of at least one full tier, not one pick.
  • IF you are considering a reach → reach only for scarcity, never for preference. Reaching one round for the last 6+ APG source is defensible; reaching for a player you like is not.
  • IF it is the last three rounds → take upside exclusively. Floor has no value in a slot you will drop by Thanksgiving. This is where Boozer, Caleb Wilson, and Coward belong.

From the same projection snapshot, counting players in each band of the 9-cat model who clear a threshold:

STL ≥ 1.4 14 6 1 21

REB ≥ 8.0 18 10 2 30

A 12-team league needs roughly two credible sources per category per team — 24 slots. Assists (26) and steals (21) do not cover that; threes (34) and rebounds (30) comfortably do. Blocks are the exception that looks scarce but is not: five of the 26 qualifiers sit at ranks 121–200, more than any other category, which is why the tiers note says blocks stay available late. Steals are the genuinely hardest category to fix after the draft.

6. Four plans

10-team, 9-cat. Be top-heavy — replacement level is high (rank 130), so mid-round picks are nearly free and waivers are genuinely deep. Take the best three players available in rounds 1–3 without regard for shape, then read your own team and decide the punt in round 4. Do not draft a second centre before round 7; a functional blocks/FG source exists at rank 121–200. Prioritise assists and steals from rounds 4–8, because those are the two you cannot stream back.

12-team, 9-cat. Balance beats stars. The elite-to-mid ratio is lower, so the marginal star pick returns less relative to a solid round-5 player. Leave rounds 1–5 with at least one elite assists source and one elite stocks source. Commit to a punt only if your first three picks already share a drain. Deep leagues punish empty roster slots more than they punish a mediocre category — draft for coverage, then trade into strength.

10-team, points. Minutes and low turnovers, in that order. Percentages are worth nothing, so the entire Pct risk flag in 2026-27 fantasy basketball tiers can be ignored — Gobert, Daniels, and Amen Thompson all gain. Confirm the weights first: if the league uses ESPN defaults, the FGA/FTA penalties partially restore efficiency and the −2 turnover weight is severe on Dončić, Cunningham, and Trae Young. In a shallow league, chase games played hard — 78-game durability at rank 60 beats 58-game brilliance at rank 40.

12-team, points. Same logic, but availability becomes the dominant variable because you cannot paper over a missing week from a thin waiver pool. Sort your board by projected season FP, not FP/G, then apply a manual discount to anyone with an H! flag. Durable mid-round volume — Knueppel (78 GP), Bridges (80 GP), Sheppard (78 GP) — is systematically underpriced by rooms drafting off category instincts.

7. First-week waiver plan

Do this before your first matchup ends, not after.

  1. Night one, watch minutes only. Opening-week box scores are noise; rotation shape is not. Anyone who played 28+ minutes off a projected bench role goes on the watchlist immediately.
  2. Drop the right slot first. Your first cut is the last pick you made "for floor," not your worst performer. Two games is not evidence about a player; a closed rotation door is.
  3. Budget discipline (FAAB). Do not spend more than ~15% of season budget in week one. The genuinely league-winning add — a starter-role vacancy from injury — usually arrives in November, and you want the budget for it.
  4. Priority discipline (rolling list). Your waiver position is a one-time asset. Spend it only on a player who would have been drafted in the top 120 had the role been known. Everything else can wait for the free-agent pool.
  5. Fill the scarce category, not the empty slot. If you left the draft light on steals or assists, week one is your cheapest chance to fix it — those are the two the wire dries up on first.
  6. Use the IL slot the day it opens. An unused IL slot is a wasted roster spot. Confirm the designation qualifies (Yahoo IL takes INJ/OFS only).

Open questions

  • Which of these rules survives contact with an actual ADP? Every "reach" and "wait" judgement here is calibrated against this vault's board, not against a market price that did not exist at the cutoff.
  • The tier-break parameter \(q\) is a judgement input, not a measured quantity. It should be estimated from real draft logs — position-scarcity tiers almost certainly run higher than 0.3.
  • Is the 10-team/12-team top-heaviness conclusion robust to the points formula? It was derived from one Yahoo-default weighting; ESPN's shot-event terms could compress or widen the elite-to-mid gap.
  • The games-played-cap section is structurally correct but untested against a specific league, and cap effects interact with the (unreleased) schedule in ways this note cannot yet quantify.
