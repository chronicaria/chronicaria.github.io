Positional scarcity as a supply constraint

Scarcity is a fact about the shape of a position's supply, not about how good its best player is — and in football the supply is constrained by how many roles exist, not by how much talent exists.

The phrase is used loosely, and here is what it should mean

"Positional scarcity" gets said about any position whose best player is very good. That is the wrong reading and it leads to expensive mistakes.

Scarcity is not about the top of a position. It is about how quickly the position runs out. Formally: line every player at a position up by projected points and look at the shape of the curve from the top down to the point where the position becomes free (replacement level). A position where that curve falls off a cliff is scarce. A position where it slopes gently is abundant — no matter how large the number at the very top is.

This is why a position can simultaneously score the most raw points in fantasy and be the cheapest thing on the board. Height of the curve is one question; steepness is another; only steepness is worth paying for.

Demand is fixed, and it is fixed by the lineup

The demand side is the easy half, because it is written down in the rules and never moves.

Twelve teams each start a fixed number of players at each position, every week, all season. Nobody can choose to start three quarterbacks because quarterbacks put up big numbers. Demand at each position is league size times slots per team, and it does not flex by a single seat between the draft and the final week. The only wobble is the FLEX slot (a lineup spot that accepts a running back, receiver or tight end), and even that is fixed in total — twelve exist; the only question is which positions win them.

Fixed demand is what makes the supply question decisive. If demand could adapt, an abundant position would simply get started more often and the imbalance would wash out. It cannot. So every difference in supply lands directly on price.

Supply: count what the league actually produces

Now count backwards from the thirty-two NFL teams, since that is the factory.

Each team fields exactly one starting quarterback. The league produces roughly thirty-two of them against twelve employed slots — nearly three times what the format can use. The overflow sits free on the wire, which is why the free option there is nearly as good as a mid-round one.

Each team feeds one lead running back, more or less. Handoffs are a single stream and most coaches concentrate it, so the league produces something close to one genuinely useful back per team — against a demand that, once FLEX is counted, exceeds two per fantasy team. Supply and demand are within shouting distance, which is exactly the condition that makes a position expensive.

Each team throws to roughly three receivers who matter, because passing is divisible in a way that handing off is not. Thirty-two times three is a large number against a demand that is real but nothing like it. Abundant.

Tight end is the odd one. Every team starts one, but most are hired to block and are never thrown to, so the count of tight ends actually used as receivers is small. The position is narrow at the top and worthless immediately after — a third shape again: a short cliff rather than a long slope.

Fixed demand, unequal supply. That is the whole engine, and every other draft principle is downstream of it.

What to do about it: reach into a cliff, never into a slope

The practical rule falls straight out of the shape.

If a position drops sharply just below the players you are considering, the cost of waiting is a step down, not a slide. Waiting one round can move you from a real asset to a commodity, and there is no way back — the players below the cliff are all interchangeable, so no amount of later cleverness recovers what you skipped. A cliff is what justifies paying above the going rate.

If a position slopes gently, waiting costs you almost nothing per round, and paying up buys you a small edge over an option you could have had for free later. Reaching into a slope is the single most common way a beginner burns a pick: it feels decisive, it is aimed at a real number, and the number it is aimed at is the height of the curve rather than its steepness.

This is also the honest content behind drafting from tiers rather than ranks — see Tiers by position. A tier boundary is a place where the curve is locally steep, so "which tier is about to run out" is "where is the next cliff," asked live.

Scarcity of talent versus scarcity of role

Here is the distinction that makes football different from every other fantasy sport, and it is the one to keep.

Scarcity of talent means there are only so many people who can do the thing well. That is the ordinary meaning, and it is mostly what scarcity means in basketball: the supply of players who can create their own scoring is genuinely small, and a team can go get more of that supply.

Scarcity of role means there are only so many jobs, regardless of who could fill them. A role is assigned by a coach, before the play, and most of the valuable roles exist exactly once per team. There is one player who gets the handoff at the goal line. One who is the offense's first look. One who is on the field on passing downs. Talent influences who is chosen; it does not create a second copy of the job.

Football's scarcity is overwhelmingly role scarcity. Thirty-two teams means thirty-two of each single-holder role and no more, and that ceiling is invisible in any list of players sorted by ability. It is also why a mediocre player in a scarce role outscores a better player without one, why the twelve Player archetypes describe jobs rather than skill, and why the touches available to a team are a fixed pot to be divided rather than something a good player can expand — see The conserved team touch budget.

The transfer warning is simple. In basketball, scarcity is something you scout. Here, scarcity is something you count.

Open questions

  • "Roughly three usable receivers per team" is a working average across very different offenses; some support one, a few support four, and the spread matters more than the mean at the margins.
  • The FLEX split between positions is assumed, and it directly sets how tight the running back and receiver markets actually are.
  • Role scarcity is stated as near-absolute. A handful of players do force a second valuable role into existence on their team, but they cannot be identified in advance, so the default holds.
