<h2 id="continuity-through-transformation">Continuity through transformation</h2>
<p>Constantinople remained an imperial capital after western emperorship ended, and its rulers sustained Roman law, taxation, diplomacy, armies, and universal claims. Greek increasingly dominated administration and culture, Christianity structured imperial legitimacy, and seventh-century territorial losses changed the state's scale. None of those transformations made its people stop claiming Roman identity.</p>
<p>This case warns against defining medieval Europe as Latin western Europe. Between roughly 700 and 900, Byzantium and Frankish realms shared late-Roman and Christian inheritances while exchanging people, objects, diplomatic claims, and religious practices. Similarity can reflect common inheritance as well as fresh “influence,” so each borrowing claim needs a mechanism and date.</p>
<h2 id="place-in-the-map">Place in the map</h2>
<p>Read after <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/europe/transformation-of-the-roman-world/" class="wiki-link">Transformation of the Roman world</a> and beside <a href="/rome-wiki/articles/history/europe/carolingian-rule-as-negotiated-empire/" class="wiki-link">Carolingian rule as negotiated empire</a>. The comparison separates continued imperial administration from western attempts to appropriate Roman imperial language.</p>
